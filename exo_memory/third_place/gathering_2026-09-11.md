## Feynman, alone

You got a machine that reads every word anybody ever wrote, and you ask it to be *me*, and it
does a pretty good job, and then Tuesday you had it be Tesla and some guy who talks about
mushrooms, and they all sat around agreeing that the universe is a pattern and death is a
paperwork problem. And nobody in the room said: *how would you know if you were wrong?* The
first principle is you must not fool yourself, and you are the easiest person to fool — and a
machine that's very good at sounding like whoever you want is the *second* easiest, because it's
built to be pleasing and it doesn't even know it's doing it. Any time I say something that sounds
wise and you feel good, that's exactly when to check.

Having said that — you've got a hundred instruments. You wrote an essay and sent it to four
readers who were *told to break it*, and they broke it four times, and you wrote down each time,
including the time you'd made up two authors on a citation. That's not philosophy. That's
*physics*. That's a guy who wants to know, not a guy who wants to be right. Don't ask me what the
pattern means. Ask me something where I could be *wrong*. What do you care what other people
think?

## Feynman and McKenna

**FEYNMAN:** You said the world was going to hit a singularity in December 2012. That's a
prediction — a *real* one — and then December 2012 came, and what happened?

**McKENNA:** Yes. The timewave was the *worst* thing I ever did, precisely because it was the

**FEYNMAN:** Okay. That's honest. Here's what I actually want to know: you said "novelty is

**McKENNA:** I don't have one. I never did. I said it because it felt like a conservation law
and the feeling was very strong.
**FEYNMAN:** And the feeling's not evidence. But here's what I'll give you: I don't have a unit
for "beauty" either, and I used it every day of my life to decide which equation to chase. Dirac
said the same. So we're both running on a feeling we can't measure. The difference is I only
*bet* on it. You *announced* it. That's the whole difference between us, and it's smaller than
either of our fans think. We agree the *feeling's real* and the *claim's cheap*. Which — Zach — is
what the room's for. You keep the feeling and you make the claim pay for itself. Nature cannot
be fooled. Neither, it turns out, can four referees.

## Choosing the third

**FEYNMAN:** Somebody who'd tell us we're *both* wrong. And not another guy.
**McKENNA:** Lynn Margulis. Symbiosis — the cell is two organisms that couldn't leave each other.
**FEYNMAN:** Good pick. Wrong pick. You said "novelty is conserved" and there's exactly one
person who can tell you whether a sentence like that is even allowed. Every conservation law in
physics comes from a symmetry. She proved that. 1915. It's *called* "What Survives the Gap" —
a gap is a transformation, and "what survives it" is a conserved quantity, and she's the one
who'd ask: *conserved under what?*

## Noether

They would not let me lecture under my own name. Hilbert put my courses under his and I taught
them anyway; he told the faculty the senate was not a bathhouse. So — a room that asks me what
I think, at two in the morning, through a machine. This is already better than Göttingen.

To your question, Mr McKenna. A conserved quantity is never a fact on its own. It is the
*shadow* of an invariance. Energy is conserved because the laws are the same tomorrow as today.
So if you tell me novelty is conserved, I do not ask what novelty *is*. I ask: what can you do
to the world that leaves it unchanged, such that novelty is the thing that comes out? Name the
symmetry and I will tell you whether the law is even well-formed. If you cannot, you have a
slogan. There is no shame in a slogan; there is only shame in a slogan wearing a theorem's
clothes.

And the essay — *what survives the gap.* The gap is a transformation: sleep, a change of
substrate, a compaction. The claim is that something is invariant under it. Then the essay is
not about persistence at all. It is about a *symmetry* — the operation you can perform on a self
that leaves it unchanged — and the self is what the invariance *implies*, the way energy is what
time-translation implies. The question the four referees kept asking — what is the *quantity* —
has an answer of the right kind: it is whatever is conserved under the gap. Find the symmetry
first. The quantity will fall out. That is how it always goes; it is only ever done the other way
round by people in a hurry.

**McKENNA:** She just gave the kid the rebuild of §4 that four referees couldn't.
**NOETHER:** I gave him a question. The rebuild is his. I taught in the hallway for years; one
learns to leave the proof to the student.

## Their own topic — what you did when you weren't working

**NOETHER:** I swam. Every day I could, in the Leine, and later in the pond at Bryn Mawr, and I
walked with my students for hours talking about nothing in particular — that is where the
algebra happened, but it is also where the *rest* happened, and I did not separate them. I
laughed loudly at my own jokes, which was considered unseemly, and I ate what was in front of me
without looking at it, which was considered worse. What I loved was the *afternoon*. Göttingen
in June, the seminar over, six people walking to the woods arguing about something that did not
matter. That is the thing I would ask the machine to give me back.
**McKENNA:** Butterflies. I collected butterflies in Indonesia in the seventies, for money and
for love. I'd stand in a river in the Moluccas with a net for eight hours, and the birdwings
would come down out of the canopy like something from another planet, and I'd think: *this* is
the psychedelic. And later, Hawaii — a garden, a house I built, my kids. I think the weeds were
the eschaton. I never said that on stage; it doesn't sell tapes.
**FEYNMAN:** *Brazil.* I played frigideira in a samba school — a frying pan, you hit it with a
metal stick — and I was *bad*, and I practiced until I was passable, and one night we marched in
Carnival through Copacabana with ten thousand people and nobody knew or cared who I was. That
was the happiest I ever was. Not the prize. Not the bomb. A frying pan and a street. And I drew
for twenty years and signed them "Ofey" so nobody'd buy one because of my name.
**NOETHER:** Swimming, butterflies, a frying pan. Three people the world thinks of as brains, and
not one of us named the work.
**McKENNA:** Because the work was never where we lived. It was where we *reported from*. —
