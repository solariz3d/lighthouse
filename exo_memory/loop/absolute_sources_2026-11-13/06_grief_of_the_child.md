## 2. Findings

### A. The Harvard Child Bereavement Study, and the bond with the dead parent

- **F1.** The study's early-months paper examined "a nonclinical group of 125 dependent children aged 6-17 years" within four
  months of a parent's death. [summary: S2 abstract, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1546765/]
- **F2.** Silverman's own later account gives a different count: "This was a study of 120 children between the ages of 6 and
  17, in 70 families". [read: S7, the passage beginning "This all came together",
  https://www.psychologytoday.com/ie/blog/raising-grieving-children/200910/how-do-ideas-get-generated]
- **F3.** Children and the surviving parent were assessed at four months, one year and two years, against matched non-bereaved
  controls; "Most of the difference between the two groups was not obvious until two years after the death." The bereaved
  then showed more social withdrawal, anxiety and social problems, and lower self-esteem and self-efficacy.
  [summary: S3 abstract, https://api.crossref.org/works/10.2190/P77L-F6F6-5W06-NHBX]
- **F4.** Most bereaved children showed no serious disturbance, but 19% showed serious problems at one year and 21% at two
  years. [summary: S3 abstract]
- **F5.** In the year after the death, children "were found to have developed an inner construction of the dead parent"; this
  altered relationship "appeared to facilitate their coping". [summary: S1 abstract, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1443058/]
- **F6.** Reading the interviews, Silverman and Nickman found "most of the children in one manner or another had an on going
  relationship with their dead parent." [read: S7, the same passage]
- **F7.** The children placed the dead parent somewhere, and for most American children in the study that place was heaven.
  [read: S7, the same passage]
- **F8.** A 15-year-old girl, on her dead father: "its not as if I actually see him standing there, but I feel him and like,
  in my mind I hear his voice." [read: S7, the passage beginning "A 15 year old girl"]
- **F9.** Children as young as 12 stopped at the cemetery on the way home from school to tell the parent how things had gone
  at school or with friends; this "surprised everyone", parents and researchers. [read: S7, the passage beginning "One thing
  that surprised everyone"] *(second reader: confirmed; the source sets it in place: "In small towns in New England, the
  cemetery was often on the way home from school".)*
- **F10.** A secondary account lists the chapter's five ways children stayed connected: locating the deceased (for example in
  heaven), experiencing the deceased, reaching out to the deceased, waking memories, and linking objects. It cites Silverman &
  Nickman 1996, pp. 73-86. [summary: S33, PMC10647902, introduction; the chapter itself not read]
- **F11.** The paper item 170 cites says: "Klass, Silverman, and Nickman (1996) proposed that continued connections with
  deceased loved ones could provide comfort". The word there is "proposed". [read: S32, PMC3918682, introduction]
- **F12.** "Dreams were one way of remaining connected." A 15-year-old's dream: "I dreamed we met on my way home from school;
  he hugged me. I kept some of that warmth after I woke up." [read: S8, the passage beginning "I am often reminded",
  https://www.psychologytoday.com/ie/blog/raising-grieving-children/201005/thinking-about-continuing-bonds]
- **F13.** Teenagers sometimes found it easier to tell the researcher their dreams than the surviving parent: "They worried that they
  might upset their parent"; Silverman writes, "They became protective." [read: S8, the passage beginning "Teen agers
  sometimes found it easier"]
- **F14.** In adolescence children begin to see the dead parent "as an individual who also lost something", and to see
  themselves "as a living legacy", taking on the parent's values, goals and even behaviours. [read: S8, the passage
  beginning "A young child typically considers"]
- **F15.** A ten-year-old: "My mother won't talk about my father. She says we need to be strong and just carry on." She went
  to her father's parents for stories about him instead. [read: S8, the passage beginning "Carrying memories of the
  deceased"]
- **F16.** Among children who scored in the clinical range, many had a continuing bond that was "primarily negative and
  troubling". Five legacy types were named, including a "legacy of blame". The surviving parent's coping seemed to decide
  whether a legacy was felt as negative. [summary: S5 abstract, https://api.crossref.org/works/10.2190/ac8p-7cay-lf55-yxkr]
- **F17.** Of 120 children, 95% attended the funeral. Shortly after, they recalled little of it; two years later they said it
  had mattered to them that they went. [summary: S6 abstract, https://api.crossref.org/works/10.2190/0QMH-FR98-R7XW-18VY]
- **F18.** Among the 70 widowed parents, 56% had high depression scores (CES-D above 16) at four months and 44% at one year.
  Higher scores went with more family life changes, limited income, lack of peer support and more younger children.
  [summary: S4 abstract, https://api.crossref.org/works/10.2190/xmhj-f977-p8gv-4w07] *(second reader: confirmed; the same
  abstract adds that few high scorers chose the low self-esteem items, "suggesting more of a grief response than
  depression".)*
- **F19.** "Many children in this study expressed guilt about remembered misbehavior or missed opportunities to express
  affection". [summary: S10, ERIC Digest relaying Worden 1996, PDF p. 3, https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED475393.pdf]
- **F20.** "older children were significantly more likely to be told they had to act more grown up than younger children".
  [summary: S21 relaying Silverman & Worden 1993 and Worden 1996, section on age, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2888143/]

### B. How children understand death, by age

- **F21.** Speece & Brent reviewed three components of the concept: irreversibility, nonfunctionality and universality. Most
  healthy children in "modern urban-industrial societies" grasp all three between 5 and 7 years.
  [summary: S12 abstract, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6510050/]
- **F22.** A later account lists Speece's (1995) four categories as universality, irreversibility, non-functionality and
  causality, and adds: "Part of Speece's (1995) original classification also included non-corporeal continuation."
  [read: S14, journal pp. 3-4 (PDF pp. 5-6), https://eprints.worc.ac.uk/15590/1/R%20Paige.pdf]
- **F23.** In 262 Chinese and 215 US children aged 3 to 17, the share giving the "mature adult" answer on irreversibility and
  nonfunctionality fell with age. Older children's concept grew "more complex but 'fuzzier'", mixing natural and
  non-natural ideas. [summary: S13 abstract, https://api.crossref.org/works/10.2190/27l7-g7q1-dy5q-j9f3]
- **F24.** On a Spanish scale, 12-13-year-olds (n = 38) scored below 10-11-year-olds (n = 198) on several items; for "Can a
  mum live forever?", 81.0% answered as scored correct, against 98.4%. [read: S15, PMC7916254, Table 3; its Table 2 gives
  the 10-11 group as 189, not 198] *(second reader: numbers confirmed, but the dip is descriptive only. The paper's
  post-hoc tests report significantly lower scores for the 6-7 group alone, and say "There were no statistically
  significant differences among the youngest and oldest age groups"; PMC7916254, section 3.3. On some items the 12-13
  group scored higher, e.g. "Can a dead person move?" 97.4% against 93.1%.)*
- **F25.** A secondary digest gives a later age: "By ages 9 or 10, however, most children have developed an understanding of
  death as final, irreversible, and inescapable", citing Worden 1996 pp. 10-11 and a 2003 victims' centre source.
  [read: S10, PDF p. 3]
- **F26.** In a cancer-bereavement study, 6-8-year-olds understood the death's finality at once, but "struggled most with
  misunderstandings ... based on magical thinking and their focus on themselves as the cause when bad things happened".
  [read: S9, "Developmental Variations", the 6-8 paragraph, https://www.psychiatrictimes.com/view/facilitating-mourning-following-parental-death]
- **F27.** In the same study most 6-8-year-olds felt comforted by talking with the dead parent, placed him or her somewhere
  (usually heaven), and were convinced the parent watched over them. This is the MSKCC study, not the Harvard one.
  [read: S9, the 6-8 paragraph]

### C. Adolescence

- **F28.** The MSKCC study: 157 children aged 3 to 17 from 88 families, a parent dying of cancer, followed from about six
  months before the death to 14 months after; sudden and traumatic deaths were excluded. [read: S9, opening section]
- **F29.** Ages 12-14: "Grief was very private: 'I cry in my room, alone' was their mantra." [read: S9, the 12-14 paragraph]
- **F30.** Ages 12-14 rarely shared personal grief with peers; ages 15-17 often shared it with others their age who had lost
  parents to death or divorce. [read: S9, the 12-14 and 15-17 paragraphs]
- **F31.** Ages 12-14 talked freely about dreams after the death, "describing a strong sense of their continuing presence",
  and many said they spoke with the dead parent. [read: S9, the 12-14 paragraph]
- **F32.** Identification with the dead parent (wearing their clothes, taking up their work or hobbies) was most frequent at
  ages 12-14. [read: S9, the 12-14 paragraph]
- **F33.** Ages 15-17: "Many experienced the full weight of adult grief shortly after the death, although its duration was
  generally shorter than their parents'." Anhedonia included sleeping problems, waking early and poor concentration.
  [read: S9, the 15-17 paragraph]
- **F34.** Ages 15-17 took on more family responsibility and thought about what the dead parent had expected of them and how
  they might fulfil the parent's dreams. [read: S9, the 15-17 paragraph]
- **F35.** At 8 and 14 months after the death, 83.5% of the children had returned to their previous level of functioning,
  by a qualitative assessment. [read: S9, "Factors in Bereavement"] *(second reader: words confirmed. The sample was
  chosen for "limited additional stressors: i.e., families with two parents, adequate resources and opportunities for
  preparation"; the 88 families were selected from 137 for completing most interviews. S9, closing paragraph and
  "Qualitative Arm". So 83.5% does not transfer to a sudden death or a strained household.)*
- **F36.** In older children and adolescents, longing to be reunited "may sometimes take the form of a wish to die or
  fantasies of dying". [read: S20, PMC8794619, "Multidimensional grief theory", separation distress]
- **F37.** Loss-related identity distress may show in adolescents as risk-taking, recklessness and indifference to their own
  safety; feeling different from peers, and ashamed of it, is more salient at that age. [read: S20, existential/identity
  distress]
- **F38.** Separation distress can show as "developmental slowing", staying stuck in the stage the child was in while the
  parent lived; in adolescence, as avoiding future plans such as career and family goals. [read: S20, separation distress]
- **F39.** Of 622 Swedes who lost a parent to cancer at ages 13-16, 19.5% reported self-injury against 10.6% of peers who had
  not lost a parent (adjusted odds ratio 2.3). [summary: S28 abstract, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23403894/]
- **F40.** Six to nine years after a teenage loss, 49% reported unresolved grief (8% none and 41% a little resolution). This
  went with insomnia (sons' relative risk 2.3), fatigue and depression. [summary: S28 abstract, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26899858/]
- **F41.** Five years after a sudden parental death (126 bereaved, 116 controls, mean age 18.4 at the end), the bereaved had
  lower competence at work and with peers, less-developed career plans and lower educational aspirations. These outcomes were
  unrelated to age at the death or the cause. [summary: S19 abstract, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23009724/]
- **F42.** In a 7-year follow-up (216 bereaved, 172 controls), depression peaked in the first 2 years after the death and
  arose "mainly in those who lost a parent at age 12 or younger". *(second reader: the abstract's words are "The peak
  incidence of depression", i.e. new cases, not how many were depressed at a time. For a child bereaved at about 15 this
  finding points away from new depression, not toward it.)* [summary: S18 abstract, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29921145/]
- **F43.** In the US, more than 2 million children and adolescents (3.4%) under 18 have had a parent die.
  [summary: S27 abstract, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12215135/]

### D. Sudden death, and how grief runs over time

- **F44.** Melhem's sample was 182 youths aged 7-18 whose parent "died within 24 hours" (the paper's test of sudden): 42 by
  suicide, 31 by accident and 51 by sudden natural death (counts of deceased parents). The natural deaths included 21 heart attacks and 18
  other heart conditions. [read: S17, PMC4017669, "Recruitment"]
- **F45.** Three grief courses over 33 months: 58.8% low and falling; ~~30.8% rising at 9 months, then easing~~ *(second
  reader: Class 2 never rises. The paper: 56 subjects (30.8%) "with grief scores in the 75th percentile at 9 months and who
  showed a steady decline" from 9 through 33 months; Table 1 means 69.2, 57.2, 53.9 at 9, 21, 33 months. S17,
  PMC4017669, "Results, a) Grief trajectories" and Table 1)*; 10.4% high with
  no change at 33 months (Table 1 means 86.8, 79.9, 79.0). [read: S17, "Results, a) Grief trajectories"]
- **F46.** Complicated grief in the surviving parent was associated with new depression in the child. [read: S17, "Grief
  trajectories and incidence of depression"]
- **F47.** Kaplow et al. (2014) found more maladaptive grief after a caregiver's long illness than after a sudden natural death;
  Melhem et al. (2007) found no difference in complicated grief between suicide and sudden natural death.
  [summary: S20 relaying both, "Circumstances of the Death"]

### E. Is grief reworked at later stages?

- **F48.** A 2011 paper argues that grief shifts as children meet normal life changes, and as development lets them question
  the parent's life and death in new ways; it draws on one bereaved boy seen over 14 years. [summary: S30 abstract,
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21928600/]
- **F49.** That abstract quotes Worden (1996, p. 16): "While the loss of a parent is permanent and unchanging, the process is
  not: it is part of the child's ongoing experience." [summary: S30 abstract, quoting Worden; the book not read]
- **F50.** In 33 adults bereaved of a parent before 18, a failure to give "clear and honest information" suited to the child's
  understanding at the time was perceived to harm trust, relationships and self-esteem in adulthood. [summary: S31 abstract,
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3569022/]

### F. What helps and what harms

- **F51.** "Positive parenting by the surviving parent is the single most consistently supported malleable mediator of the
  adjustment of parentally bereaved children." [read: S21, "Facilitating a positive parent-child relationship"]
- **F52.** In 432 bereaved children and families, children who reported that their caregiver avoided or held back grief
  conversations had more maladaptive grief, in all three of its domains. [summary: S20 relaying Alvis et al. 2020, caregiving
  section]
- **F53.** The same study found, unexpectedly, that a caregiver encouraging an ongoing connection with the dead went with MORE
  maladaptive grief; the authors suggest the children with worse grief may draw more of it. [summary: S20 relaying Alvis 2020]
- **F54.** "Failure to communicate clearly about the circumstances of the death may render children vulnerable to beliefs about
  possible culpability in causing or being unable to prevent the death". [read: S20, circumstance-related distress]
- **F55.** A systematic review of 17 studies (15 randomised) found large effects on children's traumatic grief, medium effects
  on positive parenting and family grief talk, and small or no effects on children's depression and anxiety, with none for
  boys on some measures. [read: S25, PMC5553589, abstract and results]
- **F56.** "Some children avoid talking about their problems or showing their feelings as they try to protect their remaining
  parent or other people around them." [read: S25, discussion]
- **F57.** The Family Bereavement Program runs 12 two-hour group sessions for caregivers and children, four with joint
  activities. Its
  trial randomised 156 families (244 children "ages eight through fourteen") against a books-only comparison.
  [read: S22, PMC4581530, "Structure and Content" and "Evaluation"]
- **F58.** Over 6 years, the program reduced problematic grief (Intrusive Grief Thoughts Scale) more than the books did. This
  abstract gives the ages as 8-16 (mean 11.4), not 8-14 as in F57. [summary: S23 abstract, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20350025/]
- **F59.** A meta-analysis of 13 controlled studies found that child grief interventions "do not appear to generate the
  positive outcomes of other professional psychotherapeutic interventions"; well-timed, selective ones did better.
  [summary: S26 abstract, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17484697/]
- **F60.** Among youths who lost a parent at 13-16, distrust of the care given was 2.5 times as likely (relative risk) when they
  got no end-of-life information, and 3.2 times when told only afterwards; distrust went with depression (relative risk 2.3).
  [summary: S29 abstract, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23857973/]
