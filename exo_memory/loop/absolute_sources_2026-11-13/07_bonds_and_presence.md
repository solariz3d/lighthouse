## 2. Findings

### Sensed presence in ordinary bereaved adults: how common, what form

F1. Rees interviewed 293 widowed people (227 widows, 66 widowers), 80.7 % of all widowed residents of one mid-Wales practice
area; 137 (46.7 %) had had hallucinations or illusions of the dead spouse, and 106 (36.1 %) still had them when
interviewed. [read] R1, p.37 Summary and p.38 Results.

F2. The forms in Rees's sample: feels the presence 115 (39.2 %); sees 41 (14.0 %); hears 39 (13.3 %); speaks to the dead
spouse 34 (11.6 %); is touched 8 (2.7 %). Presence is the most common form and touch the rarest. [read] R1, p.38 Table IV.
*(second reader: the numbers are confirmed in Table IV and its text, p.38: "The least common hallucination is the feeling of
being touched by the dead spouse (2.7%)". The base is all 293 interviewed. Sources disagree on the ranking: in the
self-selected sample of R7 (Table 1) touch is 47.8 %, sight 46.4 %, hearing 43.4 % and presence 34.3 %, and the authors
write that presence "was relatively uncommon" there, which they put down to method. [read] R7, Table 1 and the paragraph
after it, https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=8388006)*

F3. Rees counted night-time experiences in bed as dreams, not as presence: "Experiences occurring in bed at night, other
than those occurring immediately after retirement, were discounted and recorded as dreams." [read] R1, p.38 "Interview".

F4. In Rees, 94 (68.6 %) were helped by the experience, 8 (5.9 %) found it unpleasant and 35 (25.5 %) neither (sum 137).
[read] R1, p.40 "Help from hallucinations".

F5. Only 38 (27.7 %) had ever told anyone, and "No doctor had been informed"; the most common reason given was fear of
ridicule (14 people). [read] R1, p.40 "Previous disclosure of hallucinations".

F6. Rees found no link with depression: treated depression in 17.5 % of those with the experiences against 18.0 % of the
rest. [read] R1, p.40.

F7. The experience was more likely with a longer and a happy marriage and with parenthood. All 11 widows who called their
marriage unhappy had none. People widowed before 40 were the least likely (P < 0.05). [read] R1, p.39 "Marital
harmony", "Age when widowed", "Childless marriages", and p.41 Table XI. *(second reader: "Age when widowed" and its
P < 0.05 are on p.38, not p.39. The marriage-length result is mixed in the source: the chi-square on Table XI "is not
significant", while the regression is "significant at 5%", in a subsidiary sample of 127. [read] R1, pp.38 and 41.)*

F8. Rees lists "Sudden" death (39.6 % of cases) among the factors that did not change the rate. [read] R1, p.40 Table VIII
(OCR of a scanned table; the value reads "39 60"). *(second reader: the table's title says "Percentages of People involved
are given in Parentheses", so 39.6 % is the share of the interviewed whose spouse died suddenly, not a rate of
experiences. Rees's "hallucination" covers all forms, not presence alone. [read] R1, p.40 Table VIII title.)*

F9. Rees's conclusion: "These hallucinations are considered to be normal and helpful accompaniments of widowhood." [read]
R1, p.37 Summary.

F10. Kamp et al. give "47% to 82% across several studies" for having at least one such experience. [read] R2,
Introduction.

F11. Castelnovo et al. give a lower range, "ranging from 30% to 60%" among widowed people. [summary: abstract] R11.

F12. In a 2025 study of 268 US adults who had lost a partner within two years, 50.7 % had a waking experience of the
deceased in the past month and 73.5 % dreamed of them. [summary: PsyPost via a summarising fetch] R15.
*(second reader: 268, "within the past two years", 50.7 % and 73.5 % are all in the raw text of the PsyPost article,
https://www.psypost.org/most-bereaved-people-dream-of-or-sense-the-deceased-study-finds-and-the-two-may-be-linked . It is

F13. The 2019 meta-analysis that put prevalence at 56.6 % (Kamp & Due, J Affect Disord 243:463-476) is retracted (notice:
J Affect Disord 250:447). [summary: PubMed records] R16.

F14. "SED occur across cultures", "in all age groups", in all kinds of relationship loss, whatever the religion, and after
natural or violent death. [read] R2, "Who Experiences SED?".

F15. Kamp's table of forms runs from a presence placed in space ("It was as if he was sitting next to me really") to a
vague felt presence, a voice, sounds such as footsteps, a full or partial figure, a touch, a smell (cigar smoke, a face
cream) and a taste. [read] R2, Table 2.

F16. Bereavement voices differ from voices in psychosis: they are "seldom anonymous and are instead clearly linked to the
hearer's biography and past relationships". [read] R2, "SED in Relation to Psychosis".

F17. Presence can sharpen absence: "a positive SED may involve (or be followed by) a painful feeling of absence, which
could be intensified when the experience ends or fails to reoccur when wanted." [read] R2, "Welcome, Unwelcome, and
Ambivalent Experiences".

F18. A sensed presence also appears outside grief, in survival situations: "a presence might be experienced as guiding one
to safety during a test of endurance such as a long-distance swim in adverse conditions" (citing Suedfeld & Geiger, and
Geiger, The Third Man Factor, 2009). [read] R2, same section, refs 86-87.

F19. The contrast case is sleep paralysis. It shares the domestic setting and the sense of intention but "often involves
malevolent presence, with anonymous identity". [read] R2, same section.

### Continuing bonds after 1996, and its critics

F29. ~~Twenty years of studies did not settle it:~~ *(second reader: the abstract gives no span of years. It describes a
shift in theory "Across the course of the 20th century" and then reviews the evidence. PMID 16187475)* "neither is it
possible to conclude that continuing nor that relinquishing bonds is generally helpful." [summary: abstract] R17 (Stroebe
& Schut 2005).

F30. Using the dead spouse's possessions for comfort went with more distress and a slower fall in grief symptoms, while
fond memories went with less distress (70 midlife bereaved). [summary: abstract] R17 (Field et al. 1999).

F31. A Dutch prospective study (56 people) weighted it the other way: possessions only weakly predicted grief and
depression, while recovering memories strongly predicted grief; the authors say this partly contradicts earlier findings.
[summary: abstract] R17 (Boelen et al. 2006).

F32. Externalised bonds (illusions, hallucinations) went with violent death and with feeling responsible for the death.
Internalised bonds (the dead as a secure base) went the other way and with personal growth (502 people). [summary:
abstract] R17 (Field & Filanosky 2010).

F33. Four later studies (partner, pet and prenatal loss) agreed: internalised bonds went with adaptation, externalised with
clinical distress. [summary: abstract] R14 (Black et al. 2022).

F34. The 2023 systematic review (79 of 319 screened studies) names its key finding as "CBs facilitating oscillation between
the comforting presence and absence of the deceased." [read] R3, Abstract and Discussion. *(second reader: the quotation
is in the Discussion. The count disagrees inside the source: the abstract says "79 of 319 screened studies", while the
Findings say "Initial searches identified 1319 records after duplicate removal" and "Of 236 records assessed for
full-text eligibility, 172 were excluded". Both are in the Aston PDF.)*

F35. The review's drivers of distressing bonds: lack of preparedness, "unexpected death", violent death, suicide, guilt and
blame, and "Unfinished business", which "was associated with blame, regret, and more distressing and conflictual CBs
expressions." [read] R3, "Bereavement related distress".

F36. The review reports keeping possessions "associated with heightened grief when compared with memories". [read] R3,
same section. *(second reader: the words are there, citing Boelen et al. 2006, Epstein et al. 2006 and Keser & Işıklı
2022. Sources disagree: Boelen's own abstract (F31, PMID 16972374) says recovering memories "was a strong predictor of
grief" and possessions "a weak predictor". Which of the three cited papers carries the review's claim was not checked.)*

F37. The review reads bonds as "one of the cognitive, behavioral, and psychological ways in which grief manifests, rather
than an indicator of prolonged grief disorder or complicated grief". [read] R3, Discussion.

F38. The review says bonds offer "an opportunity to express forgiveness, say goodbye, or integrate further information"
about the life and death of the deceased. [read] R3, implications for practice.

F39. Across cultures Klass sorts the dead into three relations: the family dead, who help and are helped; the hostile dead,
who threaten the living; and the political dead. [summary: abstract] R17 (Klass 2014).

### Children

F40. In a community sample, within a year of a parent's death children had built an inner construction of the dead
parent, and this "continued, though altered, relationship" seemed to help them cope. [summary: abstract] R18 (Silverman,
Nickman & Worden 1992).

F41. Teenagers kept dreams of the dead parent from the surviving parent to protect them: "They worried that they might
upset their parent if they told him or her, their dreams about their now dead parent." [read] R9, "Thinking about
continuing bonds".

F42. The bond changes with age: "As children move into their adolescent years they have a growing ability to see their
dead parent as an individual who also lost something." [read] R9, same post.

F43. ~~A child in the study, quoted by Silverman:~~ *(second reader: the post introduces her only as "A ten year old"
and does not say she was in the study; the dream examples earlier in the post are tied to the study, this one is not)*
"My mother won't talk about my father." She went to her father's parents for stories instead. [read] R9, same post.

F44. A review of 20 studies of bereaved young people (aged 24 and under) found three kinds of connection: unintended
(a spontaneous sense of presence, "watching over them"), intended (objects, photographs, places, letters) and internalised
(the parent's values taken in). [read] R5, Results. *(second reader: the manuscript's abstract says "Nineteen articles
were included", while its body and flow chart say 20; the published abstract on Crossref, doi 10.1177/0030222819853195,
says "Twenty articles". The review covers bonds with deceased family members, siblings included, not parents only.)*

F45. In one study of 24 children aged 6-17, children said they could "hear" the parent; this came earlier in the loss and
more in younger children, though a later study found age did not matter. [read] R5, "Unintended connections" (Normand,
Silverman & Nickman 1996, a chapter of the 1996 book, pp. 87-104; not opened).

F46. Objects were a link to memory, a "bridge between the world with and world without one parent"; photographs,
jewellery and clothing were common. [read] R5, "Intended connections".

F47. Some surviving parents disposed of or hid such objects, and "the premature disposal of mementos may create additional
challenges for a bereaved young person". [read] R5, "Intended connections".

F48. In 58 parent-bereaved young people, "the most treasured objects and mementos were those that were specifically
created by a parent before death". [read] R5, "Intended connections" (Saldinger et al. 2004).

F49. Memory boxes give young people "an important sense of control regarding where and when memories of the deceased are
evoked". [read] R5, "Intended connections" (Wood et al. 2012).

F50. Nine adolescents described the dead parent as an "inner guide", replaying the parent's sayings. [read] R5,
"Internalised connections" (Hansen et al. 2016).

F51. The surviving parent shapes how a child (aged 10-12) builds a connection to the dead parent; some parental attitudes
help and some do not. [summary: abstract] R18 (Nickman, Silverman & Normand 1998).

F52. Bereaved siblings (39, mean age 12.28 years) most often kept the dead child's toys and belongings (44 %); 28 % of
siblings reported comfort, against 86 % of mothers. These are answers to one open question, so they are mentions, not
prevalence. [read] R6, Participants and Results.

### Kept objects

F53. Volkan, quoted in the foreword, framed linking objects clinically: with introjects they let mourners "keep in touch
with the images and representations of the dead and to maintain the illusion of absolute control", and their existence
"provides important clues in the diagnosis of established pathological mourning" (line-break hyphen removed). [read]
R10, Foreword, p.xviii.

F54. Of 294 mothers whose infants died of SIDS, 98.6 % kept such objects, and 63.2 % visited them more than once a week.
[read] R4, Results.

F55. The objects cut both ways: 35.1 % reported high comfort from them and 26.4 % high distress, 5.1 % both. [read] R4,
Results 3.3.

F56. Mothers described "heartache as they noticed the smell of their child disappearing from them." [read] R4,
Introduction.

### Dreams of the dead

F57. Of 278 bereaved hospice caregivers, 58 % dreamed of the dead. ~~Most dreams were pleasant or mixed, few purely
disturbing,~~ *(second reader: the abstract counts people, not dreams: "Most participants reported that their dreams were
either pleasant or both pleasant and disturbing, and few reported purely disturbing dreams". PMID 23449603)* and 60 % felt the dreams affected their grief. Themes included the dead free of illness, at peace, and passing
on a message. [summary: abstract] R12.

F58. In 216 partner-bereaved people, dreams of the dead served three functions: processing trauma, keeping a continuing
bond, and regulating emotion. How the dreamer reacted on waking shaped both the dream's meaning and the grief. [summary:
abstract] R14 (Black et al. 2021).

F59. Domhoff reports Barrett's four kinds in 77 student dream reports: "back to life" dreams, where the dreamer is amazed
or upset to see the dead alive (39 %); advice (23 %); leave-taking or resolution (about 29 %); and talk about the nature of
death (about 18 %), of which 53 % involved telephone calls, against 3 % of a random dream sample. [read] R8.
*(second reader: all confirmed in the raw page. Two things left out: telephone calls "appeared in 24% of the dream in the
other three categories", and the fourth, telephone-heavy category usually involves someone "who was not emotionally close
to the dreamer". The 77 reports come from university students; the percentages sum past 100 % because seven reports fit
two or more categories.)*

F60. Leave-taking dreams "almost always occur after back-to-life dreams" in a dream series, and their tone is extremely
positive. [read] R8.
