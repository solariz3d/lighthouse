## 2. Findings

### The Babadook (Jennifer Kent, 2014)

F1. Kent names the film's subject as the blocking of grief, not grief itself: "The film's actually about suppression
rather than grief, because the grief is not felt." [read] S1, ~~pull-quote under the photo caption "Screen Australia"~~
*(second reader: the words are found, but only as a display pull-quote set above the photo credit "Screen Australia";
the sentence does not occur anywhere in the transcribed Q&A of S1, so it is the outlet's excerpt, not a checked answer)*.

F2. Kent says the film began in her own bereavement and one question: "How painful would it be if you couldn't feel
that?" [read] S1, her answer to the question on mental illness.

F3. Kent says the genre was chosen because the story would fail as drama: "Can you imagine this story as a domestic
drama? It would be so melodramatic and stupid." [read] S2, answer on why horror.

F4. Kent says the horror was always subordinate to the hidden subject: "The horror always had to be subservient to the
idea, which was the suppression of dark, painful feelings." [read] S4, answer on horror tropes.

F5. The monster is not defeated, and Kent calls that crucial: "You can't kill the monster, you can only integrate it."
[read] S2, answer on the ending.

F6. Kent refused money to change the ending and calls it unfinished on purpose: "We had offers to finance the film if we
changed the ending", and "It's a partial resolution, a negotiation that's begun". [read] S3, answer on the ending.

F7. Wikipedia's plot says the monster speaks through the dead: "An apparition of Oskar appears, offering to return if
she "brings the boy" to him." It also says ~~the burned book returns~~ *(second reader: the book she "rips up and
disposes of" returns; she burns it only after it comes back. S5 "Plot": "Amelia subsequently rips up the book and
disposes of it", then "finds the Mister Babadook book reassembled", then "Terrified, she burns the book")* "reassembled"
with new pop-ups of her killing the dog, her son and herself. [read] S5, section "Plot".

F8. Wikipedia's plot ends with care, not victory: mother and son gather worms in a bowl, she takes them to the basement,
"she soothes it and it retreats to the corner", then she returns to celebrate his birthday. [read] S5, "Plot", last
paragraph.

F9. A critic names the opposite failure in the same film: "a self-help moral about how we need to learn to live with our
grief-monsters", "shaped around its message to the point that it feels a little too tidy." [read] S6, the paragraph
comparing Hereditary to The Babadook. (F57 shows a critic who disagrees.)

### A Monster Calls (Siobhan Dowd's idea, Patrick Ness's novel 2011, J.A. Bayona's film 2016)

F10. Ness frames the book as fear of loss, and the grieving child's ask as company: "it is fundamentally about fear of
loss", and "I'm not asking you to take it away, I'm just asking to not be alone." [read] S7, answer beginning "The way I
always thought about the book".

F11. Ness warns against softening: "You can write it beautifully so you feel it less. The truth in this story is the
sadness, and the anger about the fear of his loss." [read] S8, answer on "Make trouble".

F12. Wikipedia's plot says the truth is withheld through three tales and spoken once, at the climax, under pressure:
the monster calls for the truth "until Conor finally yells that he wishes it was all over." His mother then dies at
12:07, the monster's hour. [read] S10, sections "The fourth story" and the plot paragraph beginning "At the end of the
book".

F13. Ness says the repeated time was planted to turn at the end: "At the very, very end there's a question that maybe
12:07 has another meaning." [read] S8, answer to "Why 12:07?".

F14. Ness describes the film's added ending as a reveal by an object, not a line: the boy opens his mother's sketchbook
and finds the monster drawn there; "So the monster had come for her as well and they share that." [read] S9, answer on
Bayona's changes.

### Pan's Labyrinth (Guillermo del Toro, 2006)

F15. ~~Del Toro refuses to settle fantasy-or-real in words but plants objects:~~ *(second reader: in this interview
del Toro does settle it in words. S11, his answers after the interviewer's "false dichotomy": "I stand in that it's
real", and "I stand by the fantasy". It is the film that leaves it to clues; and the third clue is an event, the walls
opening, not an object.)* "The film is a Rorschach test of where
people stand", yet "there are three clues in the movie that tell you where I stand" (the flower at the end, the chalk
door, the walls that open for her). [read] S11, his answers on the "false dichotomy". *(second reader: in the source
the clues come first and the Rorschach line closes the answer; "false dichotomy" is the interviewer's phrase.)*

F16. He reads the ending as a birth, not a death: "It's a movie about a girl who gives birth to herself," and "At that
moment, it doesn't matter if her body lives or dies." [read] S12, paragraph beginning "These themes are central".

F17. Wikipedia's plot: the dying girl appears in a golden throne room where her mother invites her "to sit next to her
father", then "Ofelia smiles as she dies." [read] S13, "Plot", last paragraph.

F18. ~~Wikipedia's plot puts a father's watch on the side of the oppressor:~~ *(second reader: S13's "Plot" never
mentions a watch. The quoted words are there. S13 names a watch once, in "Effects": the captain's room "is supposed to

on a rock"; the captain answers "Nonsense. He didn't own a watch." [read], unofficial transcript.)* the captain asks

### A Ghost Story (David Lowery, 2017)

F20. The grieving wife's note is never shown, by decision: "Nothing written there would mean anything to the audience at
that point, and it would just complicate that moment". [read] S14, paragraph beginning "At the end of the film".

F21. Lowery says ~~no one on the film knows what the note says~~ *(second reader: he says he does not know; the actress
wrote something real and was never asked. S15: "The note that Rooney wrote ... was something she actually wrote. I don't
know what it says", and the prop pulled out later "was blank". Its writer may know.)* ("we never asked her what it
was. It went down with the house.") and that this is better: "if anyone saw what it said, it wouldn't be as good or as meaningful as not knowing."
[read] S15, answers on the note and on the Lost in Translation comparison.

F22. The film's one long look at grief is a private act watched by the ghost: "I wanted to depict grief and I also
wanted to be with her in an extremely private moment." [read] S16, answer on the pie scene.

F23. Where the film does say its theme aloud, the director deflates the speaker: "We wanted him to take himself very
seriously, but for the movie not to take him quite as seriously." He adds that what is said is still "truthful". [read]
S18, answer on Will Oldham's monologue.

### Silent Hill 2 (Team Silent, 2001)

F24. Its makers aimed at tears as well as fear: "Obviously, we want to scare the player, but we'd also like it if the
player were to cry at some of the more emotional scenes." [read] S20, ~~Sato's last answer~~ *(second reader: his
5th of 13 answers, the one beginning "We're trying to combine horror and a dramatic story"; the last answer is about
staff who "didn't grow up playing games")*.

F25. They said the endings change meaning more than events: "These decisions tweak your perception of the story,
though, more than actual events." [read] S21, answer on story progression.

F26. The ending is chosen by hidden counts of small behaviour, never a menu. The fan record says the counts start uneven
(Leave 5, Maria 3, In Water 2 points) and lists, for "In Water": "James must act in a suicidal or reckless manner while
also keeping a strong attachment to Mary", read from low health, examining a knife, reading a diary and listening to the
sickbed conversation. [read] S22, the points section and section "In Water Ending".

F27. Wikipedia's plot: the truth arrives by an object the player finds (a videotape); when James accepts it, "The
envelope from Mary disappears"; and "Konami has kept their canonicity ambiguous" across the endings. [read] S23,
"Plot", hotel and endings paragraphs.

F28. Wikipedia says the monsters "reflect the protagonist's subconscious" and Pyramid Head "is intended to be a punisher
for James". [read] S23, "Development". *(second reader: the sentence opens "For the most part", and names the
"Creepers" as an exception; S23, "Influences and design".)*

### What Remains of Edith Finch (Giant Sparrow, 2017)

F29. Dallas says players arrive expecting to solve the mystery by the credits: "That's not what this game is." [read]
S25, paragraph beginning "As Dallas explained it".

F30. Dallas fixes only the deaths and treats their certainty as freeing: "each of these family members is dead. How and
why it happened is open to interpretation", and "Knowing that you can't stop it also means knowing that you can't fail."
[read] S26, answers on the curse and on balancing emotional themes.

F31. His name for the design target: players "knew what was about to happen, but there's a joyful inevitability to it",
"marching happily to your doom." [read] S27, answer on Gregory's story.

F32. Wikipedia: the one hopeful fact (Edith is pregnant) is available early only to a player who looks down at her
first-person body. Dallas had not wanted the body seen; a tech artist kept it, which "Dallas found later to be a
pleasant surprise for players that discovered this on their own." [read] S28, "Development", paragraph on the ending.

### Omori (Omocat, 2020)

F33. Wikipedia's plot: the death is first framed as suicide; the truth (an accidental push, then a staged hanging) is
held behind missing photos in a friend's album until near the end. [read] S29, "Plot", paragraphs on the album and the
truth.

F34. Love counts through a small repeated act: "If the player watered Basil's garden repeatedly in Headspace, an
after-credits scene will depict Basil waking up to the sight of Sunny in the hospital." [read] S29, "Endings".

F35. A critic says the late recast from grief to guilt broke the game: "the whole experience falls apart"; the true
ending treats speaking the truth "as if confession and forgiveness are the same things", whereas "forgiveness is
something given as a gift, not something we can earn by suffering enough." [read] S30, paragraphs beginning "But for
most of the game", "And by spending time" and "In its way".

F36. Another critic, who calls a mental-illness twist "cheap", says Omori's twist works because "Omori explained at the
beginning that what happened in Whitespace and Headspace was not real". [read] S31, paragraphs beginning "Using mental
illness" and "You can't do that in a game".

### Little Nightmares (Tarsier, 2017)

F37. Mervik on the withholding rule and its limit: "you don't want to be willfully vague or not give people anything to
hang their hat on. But you don't want to not trust the player at the same time." [read] S32, answer on ambiguity.

F38. He says a child protagonist is the light that makes the dark bearable: "you have a tiny glimmer of light in the
darkness. That's very important to us. Fear is nothing without a beating heart." [read] S33, answer on the child.

F39. He says deaths are ~~kept off-screen~~ *(second reader: S33 says only "we didn't want to be so explicit with things
like death animations"; "off-screen" is not in it)* because "Your imagination is way better at filling in those dark
places". [read] S33, answer on death animations.

### Inside (Playdead, 2016)

F40. Its sound director says the boy's breathing blends recorded rhythms "ranging from relaxed to panic", and the rise
and fall of his chest "is controlled by the 'breathing' audio data." [read] S35, first answer.

F41. He names death and respawn as a place games neglect: "attention to the overall experience embracing death/respawn",
since hearing "the same line or music cue over again" on each respawn annoys. [read] S35, same answer.

### Nevermind (Flying Mollusk, 2015), the reference the room already holds

F42. The player is an outsider to the trauma, and calm is the skill: "a Neuroprober, a scientist who travels inside the
sub-consciousness of severe PTSD patients to reconstruct the forgotten memory of the original psychological traumatic
event"; "If the biofeedback sensor detects psychological arousal in the player, the game will become more difficult."
[read] S37, section 2.

F43. Reynolds aims at evocation, not depiction: "to abstractly and metaphorically evoke the experience and aftermath of a
traumatic experience", and says that version is "not necessarily intended to be played by those who have undergone a
traumatic experience". [read] S38, answer on players who lived through trauma.

F44. Her own measurements found something other than fear: "we saw stronger correlations with emotions like anger or
disgust and even heightened attention levels", and "it really isn't "horror" by most video game standards." [read] S39,
section "So, what is the essence of fear in Nevermind?".

F45. The store text says the game "is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease". [read] S40,
detailed_description, disclaimer. (The store page HTML also carried a content warning for "traumatic or intense
situations".)

### Gris (Nomada Studio, 2018)

F46. A co-founder says the missing dialogue is the method: "the absence of dialogue allows us to convey a narrative while
giving players room to project their own thoughts and interpretations." [read] S42, Adrián's answer on dialogue.

F47. The studio says each level is coded to a stage of grief by colour: "Denial (grey), Anger (red), Bargaining (green),
Depression (blue), and Acceptance (yellow)." [read] S43, answer on colour.

F48. A reviewer says the beauty removed the hard parts: "the ugly and destructive sides of grief or loss are absent",
"the grief is never effectively localised to a particular person", and the ending is "too neat". [read] S44, paragraphs
beginning "As an exploration of grief", "The other big problem", "Another issue".

F49. Another critic says the open metaphor itself was the failure: "you can and should be more specific than to suggest
that a loss of some kind occurred". [read] S45, paragraph beginning "Gris' attempt to evoke emotion".

F50. A third critic says epic allegory distances the player ("the epic metaphors hold me at a remove from the text") and
that grief is not beaten by action: "you can't press X to will your sadness or bereavement away." [read] S46,
paragraphs beginning "There are major shortcomings" and "Having smaller, lower-stakes grievances". *(second reader: the
"hold me at a remove" line is said of Hellblade, The Last of Us and God of War. The next paragraph extends it: "This
distancing due to allegory happens also in smaller budget games like Gris or Spiritfarer". She also says Gris "helped me
through a rough patch" and that Spiritfarer "is more adept at balancing allegories of grief with mundane expressions".)*

F51. A games journalist says the five-stage model's use is "grounded more in popular narrative than actual truth" and
that Kübler-Ross "regretted writing it in a manner that suggested everyone deals with grief the same way". [read] S55,
second paragraph. The claim about Kübler-Ross herself was not checked at its source (section 6).

### Spiritfarer (Thunder Lotus, 2020)

F52. Its director grounded the dying characters in research, "meeting patients and caregivers in end-of-life care

## 4. Where it contradicts or strains something the room holds

- The twist as self-blame. The bible makes the darkness "the part of him that blames himself for that night", and Rescue
  "forgiving himself, never named as that" (`handback/p-d667-story-E_2026-11-11.md:389-396`). Omori's late recast of
  grief into guilt is the case a critic says broke the game, equating confession with forgiveness (F35). Against it:
  Silent Hill 2's guilt reveal is the genre's model (F27, F28), and another critic says Omori's twist works because the
  dream was declared unreal at the start (F36). The father's death is now "sudden and ordinary" with nothing to be
  guilty of (`briefs/dreamzone2_the_game.md:1353`); a guilt layer may pull against "never makes sense" (item 170).
  [inferred]
- The name that lands. Chapter 5's "[LANDS]" shows the darkness's name as the boy's own (`p-d667-story-E:273-274`).
  Den of Geek's "wearing a sign" failure (F57) and Lowery's unshown note (F20) argue for less; Silent Hill 2's explicit
  tape (F27) and A Monster Calls' single spoken truth (F12) argue that one plain statement at the very end can hold.
  Sources disagree; it is his call. [inferred]
- Grief as the key that explains the child. The bible's "the PC found him because his grief did"
  (`p-d667-story-E:371-376`) and item 171's grief as "a gateway" make the wound the source of his gift. Sehgal's
  critique is precisely the wound as the key to the self (F56). A counterweight already in the room: item 174 keeps the
  lore on the surface unless the player digs (`dreamzone3_design.md:256`), so the wound need not explain the child to a
  player who does not search. [inferred]
- The door that opens after enough Recognitions. The watch is earned behind the hospital door after Recognition "3 times"
  (`p-d667-story-E:435-440`). That rewards making sense of the loss, against item 170's "never makes sense" and Kent's
  "partial resolution" (F6); Warr calls Gris's resolving ending "too neat" (F48). [inferred]
- The good god always saves. Decided 66 and the pick "Never fail" (`dreamzone2_the_game.md:1373-1375`) make the rescue
  certain every run. Warr's "too neat" (F48) warns against endings that close grief; it does not apply directly to a
  rescue that leaves the loss in place (F5, F8 show a monster kept alive inside a loving ending). [inferred]
- The watch motif's prior use. The father's watch "Still running" (`p-d667-story-E:452-455`) shares its central object
  with Pan's Labyrinth, where a father's watch is the oppressor's inheritance, refused (F18). Not a conflict; an echo a
  player may notice. [inferred] *(second reader: stands on downgraded F18. The watch is not in Wikipedia's plot; an
  unofficial transcript has it, and there the captain himself denies the legend: "He didn't own a watch.")*
- The Nevermind reference. Item 46 recalls "a future therapist who delves into the dreams of client"
  (`dreamzone2_the_game.md:387`). In Nevermind the player visits someone else's trauma and calm is rewarded (F42); in
  this game the player is the child, and full stress can roll Resonant (decided 32, `dreamzone3_design.md:121`). The
