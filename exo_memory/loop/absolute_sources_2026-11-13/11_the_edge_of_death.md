## 2. Findings

### The measuring stick: the Greyson NDE Scale

- **F1** [read] The scale has 16 multiple-choice items in four factors: cognitive, affective, "purportedly paranormal" and
  transcendental. S3, Methods, p. 2: "The NDE scale consists of 16 multiple-choice items comprising four factors."
- **F2** [read] The 16 items, as named in S3 Table 1 (p. 3): time distortion, thought acceleration, life review, sudden
  understanding; peace, joy, cosmic unity, light; sensory vividness, ESP, precognitive visions, out-of-body experience;
  unfamiliar environment, unidentified presence, religious or deceased spirits, border or "point of no return".
- **F3** [read] The usual threshold is 7 of 32 points. S4, Results, p. 2: reports with "NDE scale total score <7/32" were
  excluded. S4 Table 2 (p. 3) gives the items' wording, e.g. "Did you come to a border or point of no return?" and "Did you
  seem to encounter a mystical being or presence, or hear an unidentifiable voice?"
- **F4** ~~[summary: search summary of the UVA scale description, PDF not opened]~~ [read] Item 16 scores 0 for no, 1 for a
  conscious decision to return to life, and 2 for a barrier the person was not permitted to cross or for being sent back
  involuntarily. *(second reader: confirmed in raw text, Pistoia et al. 2018, PMC5811479, Table 1: "16. Did you come to a
  border or point of no return?", "1 = A conscious decision to "return" to life", "2 = A barrier I was not permitted to
  cross; or "sent back" to life involuntarily", "0 = Neither". The UVA document itself was still not opened.)*
- **F5** [inferred] So a "border" count from this scale merges three different reports: reaching a border, choosing to
  return, and being sent back. None of the tables read (S2, S3, S4) splits them.

### How often, and what: prospective hospital studies

- **F6** [read] S1, Summary (p. 2039): of 344 consecutive resuscitated cardiac-arrest patients in 10 Dutch hospitals,
  "62 patients (18%) reported NDE, of whom 41 (12%) described a core experience." Depth was coded on Ring's weighted index:
  1 to 5 superficial, 6 or more core, 10 or more deep (S1, Methods, p. 2040).
- **F7** [read] S1 Table 2 (p. 2041), n = 62: awareness of being dead 31 (50%); positive emotions 35 (56%); out of body 15
  (24%); moving through a tunnel 19 (31%); communication with light 14 (23%); colours 14 (23%); celestial landscape 18
  (29%); meeting with deceased persons 20 (32%); life review 8 (13%); presence of border 5 (8%). (The layout extraction
  misaligns the last two rows; the raw extraction gives these values.)
- **F8** [read] S1, Prospective findings (p. 2041): "No patients reported distressing or frightening NDE."
- **F9** [read] S1, Introduction (p. 2039) links the three kinds of account: "Similar experiences to near-death ones can
  occur during the terminal phase of illness, and are called deathbed visions." It names "fear-death experiences" after
  situations "in which death seemed unavoidable", ~~such as falls and accidents~~ *(second reader: S1 p. 2039 lists
  "serious traffic accidents, mountaineering accidents, or isolation such as with shipwreck"; falls are not named. S1
  also calls fear-death experiences "Identical experiences to NDE", which F12's source disputes; see F12.)*
- **F10** [read] S2, Abstract (p. 269): in 1,595 consecutive cardiac admissions over 30 months, "Near-death experiences were
  reported by 10% of patients with cardiac arrest and 1% of other cardiac patients."
- **F11** [read] S2 Table 2, prospective, n = 27 experiencers: peace 23 (85%); light 19 (70%); out of body 19 (70%); altered
  time 18 (67%); joy 18 (67%); "otherworldly" environment 17 (63%); deceased or religious spirits 14 (52%); cosmic unity 14
  (52%); accelerated thought 12 (44%); border or "point of no return" 11 (41%); life review 8 (30%); sudden understanding 8
  (30%); mystical entity 7 (26%).
- **F12** ~~[read]~~ [summary: S2 reporting earlier work] S2, Discussion (p. 272): experiences without documented arrest,
  which the paper calls fear-death experiences, "tend to include fewer sensations of bright light, enhanced cognitive
  function, and positive emotions". *(second reader: the words are in S2, but S2 credits the finding to "Prior
  researchers" at its ref. [18], Owens, Cook & Stevenson, "Features of 'near-death experience' in relation to whether or
  not patients were near death"; it is not S2's own data. S2's own finding is the lower rate, F10. S1 p. 2039 calls
  fear-death experiences "Identical experiences to NDE", and S4 found NDE-like accounts without threat to life did not
  differ in content (F16). Sources disagree.)*
- **F13** [summary: abstract] S15 (AWARE): 2,060 cardiac arrests; 140 survivors at stage 1, 101 at stage 2. 46% had memories
  in 7 themes: fear; animals/plants; bright light; violence/persecution; deja-vu; family; recalling events after the arrest.
  9% had NDEs; 2% described explicit seeing and hearing of their resuscitation.
- **F14** [summary: abstract] S16 (AWARE-II): 567 in-hospital arrests; 53 (9.3%) survived; 28 interviewed; 11 (39.3%)
  reported memories or perceptions. 6/28 (21.4%) had a "transcendent recalled experience of death". Nobody identified the
  hidden visual image; 1/28 identified the sound.

### How often, and what: retrospective collections

- **F15** [read] S4 Table 2 (p. 3), 140 "real NDE" accounts after coma: peace 127 (91%); separated from the body 111 (79%);
  brilliant light 106 (76%); time sped or slowed 105 (75%); unearthly world 101 (72%); joy 98 (70%); border or point of no
  return 86 (61%); mystical being, presence or voice 71 (51%); deceased or religious spirits 54 (39%); scenes from the
  past 37 (26%).
- **F16** [read] S4, Results (p. 2): 50 "NDE-like" accounts with no threat to life came from sleep (13), fainting (11),
  meditation (5), drugs or alcohol (3) and other (18), and did not differ in intensity or content from the 140 after coma.
  The border item was reported by 31 of 50 (62%) of them.
- **F17** [read] S4 Table 3 (p. 4): deceased or religious spirits were reported by 27% (12/45) of the retrospective
  post-anoxic group and 57% (24/42) of pooled prospective post-anoxic studies (p = 0.004). Sources disagree by design here.
- **F18** [read] S4, Results (p. 3): 18/190 (10%) reported no peace, happiness or joy; 2 "explicitly recounted a negative
  experience".
- **F19** [read] S5 Table 3, 154 French written narratives: peace 123 (80%); bright light 106 (69%); encountering spirits or
  people 99 (64%); out of body 81 (53%); tunnel 73 (47%); border or point of no return 62 (40%); returning into the body 57
  (37%); entering the light 27 (18%); life review 25 (16%).
- **F20** [read] S5 Table 3 and Results (p. 4): the commonest first feature is the out-of-body experience (54, 35%); the
  commonest last feature is "Returning into the body" (56, 36%). The border is the last feature in 26 (17%).
- **F21** [read] S5, Results (pp. 5-6): by position, the border is the most frequent feature at the 5th and 6th place (22%,
  31%) and the return at the 7th (56%); all three narratives with a ninth feature ended in the return (100%).
- **F22** [read] S5 Table 1 defines the two end features. Border: "Approaching a border, a point of no return and/or a
  barrier —sometimes without access permissions." Return: "Experiencing a decision by oneself or others to return to one's
  body —often accompanied by feelings of reluctance."

### Distressing accounts, including the void

- **F28** [summary: abstract] S14 names three types: experiences like the peaceful kind but "interpreted as unpleasant";
  "a sense of nonexistence or eternal void"; "graphic hellish landscapes and entities". The first "may eventually convert to
  a typical peaceful experience".
- **F29** [read] S7, "The Void NDE": "an ontological encounter with a perceived vast emptiness, often a devastating scenario
  of aloneness, isolation, sometimes annihilation."
- **F30** [read] S7, void examples: a man attacked by a hitchhiker, "floating in nothing but black space, with no up, no
  down, left, or right"; a woman in childbirth told by circular entities that her life had never been real.
- **F31** [read] S7, inverse type: a man thrown from a horse, watching the medics, screams "Put me back!"; a woman sees a
  ball of light and feels "we were on a collision course".
- **F32** [read] S7, hellish type: a man in heart failure falls "into the depths of the Earth", finds rusty gates, and
  "managed to scramble back up to daylight".
- **F33** [read] S7, "Seven Things to Know": "as many as one in five NDEs may be predominantly distressing" (citing Bush
  2009); and "No evidence supports the conventional assumption that 'good' people get pleasant NDEs and 'bad' people have
  distressing ones."
- **F34** [read] S7: the religious element is often an absence: "I called on God and He wasn't there. That's what scared
  me." Distressing reports "typically lack" a positive tone "and loss of the fear of death".
- **F35** [summary: abstract] S18: 14% of 123 narratives were distressing: 8 inverse, 8 hellish, 1 void. Suicide survivors
  were a larger share of the distressing group.
- **F36** [summary: Psi Encyclopedia, citing Bush 2009 and Gallup & Proctor 1982] Eleven studies with 1,359 NDEs reported 315
  (23%) as distressing; across 21 studies from 1975 to 2005, 17.2%; a Gallup survey found 1% with "a sense of hell or
  torment".
- **F37** [summary: Psi Encyclopedia, quoting Grey 1985] Hellish features included "often a definite sense of being dragged
  down by some evil force" and "unseen beings or figures which are often faceless or hooded".
- **F38** [summary: Psi Encyclopedia] Its explanation for the gap between F8 and F36: distressing accounts come out with a
  trusted interviewer, late and unhurried, and "Inpatient hospital studies ... do not meet any of these conditions".
  *(second reader: the article's sentence is about "The most accurate information" in general, citing Sutherland (1995),
  54; it then applies it to distressing NDEs. Against it: the AWARE study, an inpatient study, did record "fear" and
  "violence/persecution" among recalled themes (F13), though not as NDEs.)*

### Deathbed visions: who comes, and what the dying say they are told

- **F46** [read] S8, p. 18 and the Methods chapter: the ~~1959-61~~ *(second reader: "The pilot survey, carried out in

  clear consciousness", unlike drug hallucinations. The 2006 pilot: phenomena "far broader than the traditional image of an
  apparition at the end of the bed"; patients would rather tell nurses than doctors.
