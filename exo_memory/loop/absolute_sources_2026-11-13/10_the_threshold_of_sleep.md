## 2. Findings

Grouped by subject. Where two sources disagree, both are given as separate findings and the pair is named.

### Hypnagogia and hypnopompia: the words, the forms, how common

F1 [read] The thesis says Maury introduced "hypnagogic" in 1848 (Greek *hypnos*, sleep, and *agogos*, conductor or
leader), and that experiences at the other end of sleep "have been called 'hypnopompic' (Myers, 1903)". (S9, PDF p.9.)

F2 [read] The 2023 review gives the year as 1904: the term hypnopompic "was later coined by Myers ( 1904 )". F1 and F2
disagree on the year. (S13, introduction.) *(second reader: OK. S13's reference list reads "Myers , F. W. H. ( 1904 ).
Human personality and its survival of bodily death (Vol. 2 ). Longmans" [read]; the thesis dates the coinage "Myers (1903,
p. 125)" (S9, PDF p.59) [read]. The two may date one book by different printings [inferred]; neither source says so.)*

F3 [read] Among sounds at sleep onset: "A usual auditory hypnagogic experience is the hearing of one's name being
called" and "another is the hearing of a doorbell ringing". The thesis cites Maury 1848, McKellar 1957 and Oswald 1962
for the first. (S9, PDF p.52.)

F4 [read] For the waking edge the thesis writes that hypnopompic sounds and voices ("hearing knocks on the door or
one's name being called") "are not at all unusual in this state", citing Lukianowicz 1959. (S9, PDF p.61.)

F5 [read] Some people falling asleep "hear crashing noises, bangs and explosions", which the thesis describes, citing
Mitchell (1890) through Critchley (1955), as "localized within the skull". (S9, PDF p.52.)

F6 [read] The thesis reports old surveys of children: de Manaceine (1897), 80 % of 6-year-olds and 40 % of
8-to-15-year-olds; Partridge (1898, 826 children), 58.5 % of 13-to-16-year-olds. Foulkes (1971) found sleep-onset
imagery in 61 % of interruptions of 9-to-10-year-olds and 18 % of 3-to-4-year-olds. The thesis says the older reports
"have recently been challenged" and that all these methods differ widely. ~~(S9, PDF p.16.)~~ *(second reader: the
figures are right; the place is split. De Manaceine, Partridge and "have recently been challenged" are on PDF p.15
(printed p.14); Foulkes' 61 % and 18 % and the methods paragraph are on PDF p.16.
https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/6638/3/FulltextThesis-Vol1.pdf)*

F7 [summary] In a UK telephone sample of 4,972 people aged 15 to 100, 37 % reported hypnagogic hallucinations and
12.5 % hypnopompic ones. (Ohayon, Priest, Caulet & Guilleminault 1996, *Br J Psychiatry* 169:459-467, abstract.)

F8 [summary] In a larger sample from the UK, Germany and Italy (n = 13,057), hypnagogic hallucinations were 24.8 % and
hypnopompic 6.6 %, more than half with no link to a specific pathology. Frightening ones were more often linked to
sleep or mental disorders. F7 and F8 are the same lead author and differ by about 12 points. (Ohayon 2000, *Psychiatry
Res* 97:153-164, abstract.)

F9 [read] The review's table lists Ohayon 2000 as finding that "The most frequent hypnagogic experienced mentioned is
the feeling of falling down an abyss, followed by felt‐presence experiences". A Sherwood row gives visual
experiences, falling and a presence in the room as the most frequent. (S13, Table 1.)

F10 [read] In a Norwegian population sample of 2,533, 6.8 % (n = 173) reported voices at sleep onset or offset,
12.3 % (n = 312) multimodal sleep-edge hallucinations, and 32.2 % (n = 816) "out-of-body" ones at least once in their
life. (S11, p.4, section 3.1.)

F11 [read] The 32.2 % item asks about three things at once: "I have felt like I am floating or falling, or temporarily
leaving my body". It is not a count of full out-of-body experiences. (S11, p.3, item 2.)

F12 [read] The same paper, citing Waters et al. 2016, says visual phenomena are up to 86 % of sleep-edge
hallucinations, somatic ones (weightlessness, flying or falling, "a sense of presence in the room") up to 44 %, and
voices and other sounds "(phone, doorbell, music)" up to 34 % of reported cases. (S11, p.2.)

F13 [read] The review's table lists Schmidt & Gendolla 2008: "Suppressed thoughts during the day are likely to rebound
into the hypnagogic state". (S13, Table 1.)

F14 [summary] Tetris players reported "intrusive, stereotypical, visual images of the game at sleep onset", and so did
three amnesic patients who could not remember playing. Images from older versions of the game also appeared.
(Stickgold et al. 2000, *Science* 290:350-353, abstract.)

F15 [read] One lab caught sleep-onset imagery Edison's way. Participants rested in a chair in the dark, holding a
bottle, and reported their thoughts when it fell. 61.70 % of drops yielded a sleep-onset experience, against 22.50 %
when the bottle did not drop. (S12, Results, "hypnagogia catcher".)

F16 [read] One report from that study, after the bottle fell during N1: "I saw a big cliff and I was climbing it. Then
boom, it (the bottle) fell and brought me back to reality". (S12, Fig. 1 legend, panel C.)

### Sleep paralysis: what it is, how common, when

F17 [read] During sleep paralysis people cannot make gross movements, "they are able to open their eyes and
subsequently to report accurately on events in their surroundings during the episode". (S1, p.319, citing Hishikawa &
Kaneko 1965.)

F18 [summary] One review's abstract contrasts dreams and sleep with waking hallucinations. "Sleep-related perceptions"
are "immersive and largely cut off from reality", while hallucinations are "overlaid on veridical perceptions". This
sits uneasily with F17, where people in sleep paralysis see their real room. (Waters et al. 2016, *Schizophr Bull*
42:1098-1109, abstract.)

F19 [read] Pooled across 35 studies (N = 36,533), lifetime sleep paralysis was 7.6 % in the general population, 28.3 %
in students and 31.9 % in psychiatric patients (34.6 % in panic disorder). Single studies ranged from 1.5 % to 100 %.
(S4, abstract and Results.)

F20 [summary] In a German and Italian population sample (n = 8,085), 6.2 % had at least one lifetime episode; 0.8 % had
one or more a week. (Ohayon, Zulley, Guilleminault & Smirne 1999, *Neurology* 52:1194-1200, abstract.)

F21 [read] In 1,273 Canadian undergraduates, 360 (28.4 %) reported sleep paralysis, women more often (32.4 %) than men
(21.4 %). F19, F20 and F21 show how far the figure moves with the sample and the question. (S1, p.323.)

F22 [read] Episodes are "usually brief (usually seconds to 20 minutes) with a mean duration of 6 minutes". (S5, the
section on recurrent isolated sleep paralysis.)

F23 [read] In 67 Italians with sleep paralysis, 18 % were still for less than a minute, 61 % for 1-5 minutes and 21 %
for more than five. 84 % had it on waking, 9 % while falling asleep and 8 % at both. 55 % were lying on their back.
(S6, Results.)

F24 [read] Only 13-19 % of people normally fall asleep on their back, but it was the most common position during
sleep paralysis, "over 50% of cases". Among those who always used the same position, the share rose to 75 % for
episodes in the middle or at the end of sleep. (S2, p.174; two studies, 6,730 people.)

F25 [read] In a study of shift workers, cited from Folkard et al. 1983, episodes were reported only at night, and
"Reported incidence in that study peaked at 4:00 am". (S2, p.170.)

F26 [read] Fear was "significantly less intense at the end of sleep than at the beginning or middle of sleep". The
group whose episodes came at the end of sleep also reported fewer Intruder hallucinations than all other groups. (S2,
p.174.)

F27 [summary] Among 322 Mexico City high-school students (mean age 15.9 years), 92.5 % knew the folk phrase "a dead
body climbed on top of me". 25.5 % had had sleep paralysis, with a mean age of onset of 12.5 years. In 72 % of cases
paralysis and hallucinations came together. (Jiménez-Genchi et al. 2009, *Psychiatry Clin Neurosci* 63:546-549,
abstract.)

F28 [summary] About 40 % of 635 Japanese college students had had *kanashibari*. About half reported "physical or
psychological stress" or a disturbed sleep-wake cycle just before an episode, and "Many subjects of K(+) experienced the
first episode in adolescence". (Fukuda et al. 1987, *Sleep* 10:279-286, abstract.)

### The three clusters, and the presence that comes first

F29 [read] Factor analysis of two samples gave three clusters. **Intruder** is a sensed presence, fear, and auditory and
visual hallucinations. **Incubus** is pressure on the chest, breathing difficulty and pain. **Unusual Bodily
Experiences** is floating, out-of-body experience and, in the web samples, bliss. (S1, pp.325-326, Tables 2-3, and the
abstract. The table columns were garbled in extraction, so cluster membership is taken from the text.)

F30 [read] The authors argue that a sensed presence with intense fear starts the episode. Sounds and shadows are then
read as threat: "Such sensations are frequently interpreted as approaching footsteps, whispering voices, or apparitions
that are taken to be concrete instantiations of the threatening presence". (S1, p.322.)

F31 [read] With no outside cause to confirm the threat, an apprehension "that normally might last only milliseconds may
last many seconds or even minutes during SP". (S1, pp.329-330.)

F32 [read] Fear was reported by 90 % of the student sample and 98 % of the web sample, against "only about 30% of
dreams". In both web samples, 64 % gave fear the maximum rating. Many wrote that the word "fear" "did not do justice to
the abject terror". (S1, pp.328-329 and 332.)

F33 [read] Few respondents had heard of the phenomenon before. Many "expressed astonishment and relief" on finding the
survey's questions, "because they had previously assumed that their experiences were unique". (S1, p.329.)

F34 [read] The Intruder cluster also included tactile hallucinations and "hallucinations that the bedding is moving or
being pulled". The bodily cluster included "illusions of movement, such as sitting up, getting out of bed, and walking
around the room". (S2, p.171.)

F35 [summary] In a prospective study of 383 people reporting single episodes, the authors say the links between the
sensory hallucinations were "largely explained by their common association with FP" (felt presence), and they read it
as a threat-activated vigilance system. (Cheyne & Girard 2007, *Conscious Cogn* 16:959-974, abstract.)

F36 [summary] Nielsen's reply calls this correlational. His alternative is that felt presence is "a purely spatial,
hallucinatory form" of ordinary social imagery, "often, but not necessarily, linked with threat and fear". He points to
non-fearful presences outside sleep paralysis. F35 and F36 disagree. (Nielsen 2007, *Conscious Cogn* 16:975-983,
abstract.)

F37 [summary] Electrical stimulation of one patient's left temporoparietal junction repeatedly produced the feeling that
someone was close by. The "illusory person closely 'shadowed' changes in the patient's body position and posture".
(Arzy et al. 2006, *Nature* 443:287, abstract.)

### Floating, leaving, falling and coming back

F38 [read] Floating was "rarely" passive. Respondents described "rising, lifting, falling, flying, spinning, and
swirling sensations", lifts and escalators, "being hurled through a tunnel", and "Several people reported feeling
forcibly pulled or sucked from their bodies, sometimes through the forehead and sometimes through the feet". One man
felt he was "falling out of" his body. (S1, p.331.)

F39 [read] Seventeen respondents saw themselves lying on the bed. "No one ever indicated seeing an image floating above
the bed from a position on the bed, rather what was seen was invariably one's body lying in bed." Unlike the Intruder
and Incubus experiences, some "desired to repeat" these. (S1, pp.331-332.)

F40 [read] In 11,385 online respondents with sleep paralysis, 69 % reported illusory movement and 39 % out-of-body
experiences. The most frequent single movement experience was "motor hallucinations" (sitting, standing, walking),
44 %; flying was the least, 15 %. (S3, pp.204-205.)

F41 [read] The average distance travelled in floating and out-of-body experiences was "approximately two meters, though
some were beyond the room". A patient under brain stimulation first felt "sinking into the bed" and "falling from a
height", then at higher current saw himself "lying in bed, from above". (S3, p.203, citing Blanke et al. 2002.)

F42 [read] The authors read out-of-body experience as a failure of "the pattern of activation of the neuromatrix that
signals that the body is intact". (S3, p.202.)

F43 [read] People who claim to leave their bodies on purpose often describe lying still on their back between waking
and sleep. Oliver Fox's method is "to send the body to sleep while the mind is kept awake" (Fox 1962), and such adepts
"frequently mention feelings of paralysis preceding or accompanying OBEs" (Fox 1962; Monroe 1971; Muldoon & Carrington
1969). (S3, p.202.)

F44 [read] The SPR encyclopedia says Monroe's experiences "began in 1958 during experiments with sleep learning". After
a brief sleep, a sensation "paralyzed him and made his body vibrate violently". Later "he found himself floating
against the ceiling of his bedroom" and saw himself asleep beside his wife. (S14; Monroe's book itself not opened.)

F45 [read] Italian accounts of leaving the body include: one "Felt 'sucked back' into his body"; one "Raised the head
but saw it remain in its place"; one "Sat on the bed but watched himself laying on the bed"; one "Saw his body lying
beside asking for help". Each was 1 person of 67. Out-of-body experiences overall: 11 of 67 (16 %). (S6, Table 3.)

### What is heard and seen; the cultural forms

F46 [read] Among 67 Italians: auditory hallucinations in 22 % (15/67). Single respondents "Heard the doorbell", "Heard
the bulb making noises", "Heard her family members calling", heard a deep masculine voice calling, or "light footsteps
on the roof". Visual hallucinations in 37 % (25/67), mostly shadows (male 4, female 4, genderless 8). Single reports
included "Saw alternating flashlights" and "fluorescent eyes (yellow-greenish) beneath their blanket". (S6, Results and
Table 3.)

F47 [read] In Abruzzo the *Pandafeche* is believed to be a witch, a ghost ("spirits of the deceased") or a black
cat-like creature. 42 % (28/66) feared dying from an episode. An earlier study (cited) found 18 % said a broom should
stand "bottom-up by the bedroom door". A regional tale says a brush behind the door works because "a witch gets stuck
counting the hairs of the brush" (Procacci 1989, cited). The authors write "To avoid exoticism". (S6, Introduction and
Discussion.) *(second reader: OK. Two precisions from PMC8060733, Discussion: the brush is local folklore about "witch
visitations", placed "under the pillow, or behind the door or window", and S6 adds only that "Similar remedies have been
suggested for the Pandafeche"; "To avoid exoticism" opens a sentence on what "future research should examine".)*

F48 [read] In Turkey, *Karabasan* "refers to a creature in Turkish folklore" that causes paralysis, or to the
experience itself; ~~93 % (56/59) of students called it that.~~ *(second reader: two figures were merged. S7, Results,
"Name associated with SP in Turkey": 95 % (56/59) "answered affirmatively" that they knew a name for it; "Of those, 93%
(52/56), that is, 88% (52/59) of all of those surveyed, mentioned Karabasan". PMC8060736.)* The paper cites an Egyptian finding that 48 % blamed the
*jinn*. One case went to a hodja, who wrote a protective *musqa* to wear at the neck. (S7, Results and case section.)

F49 [summary] Fewer Danes than Egyptians had had sleep paralysis: 25 % (56/223) against 44 % (207/470). Egyptian
experiencers had more lifetime episodes (mean 19.4 against 6.0), and believing the cause was supernatural went with
more fear and longer immobility. (Jalal & Hinton 2013, *Cult Med Psychiatry* 37:534-548, abstract.)

F50 [summary] In 100 Cambodian refugee psychiatric patients, 42 % had sleep paralysis in the past year. 91 % of them
saw an approaching being. The panic was heightened by cultural ideas and "by trauma associations to the figure seen".
(Hinton et al. 2005, *Transcult Psychiatry* 42:46-77, "'The ghost pushes you down'", abstract.)

F51 [read] Historical names listed for the presence that sits on the sleeper include Roman *incubus* ("one who presses
or crushes"), German *Alpdruck* ("elf pressure") and *hexendrücken* ("witch pressing"), French *cauchemar*, Spanish
*pesadilla*, and Old English *maere* and *hagge*. The authors write "night-mare" with a hyphen for sleep paralysis with
hallucinations, ~~its older sense.~~ *(second reader: the authors give this only as what others hold: "some authors have
argued that SP with HHEs constitutes the original referent of the term" nightmare.)* (S1, p.320.)

F52 [summary] Wikipedia says that in Newfoundland the experience is "the Old Hag" and the sufferer is "hag-ridden" on
waking. It lists wards: a Bible under the pillow, calling the sleeper's name backwards, and a "Hag Board" of nails
strapped to the chest. It glosses Japanese *kanashibari* as "metal binding". (S16, "Sleep paralysis", Newfoundland and
Japan sections; the cited sources were not opened.)

### Meditation at the edge

F53 [read] Jalal proposes a four-step response during an episode: reappraisal, distancing, inward focused attention,
and muscle relaxation. Step III focuses on "an emotionally salient positive object (e.g., a memory of a loved one or
event, a hymn/prayer, God)". Step IV says to relax "and under no circumstances attempt to move". It is a proposal with
case examples, not a trial. (S8, sections on MR therapy.)

F54 [read] The same paper's panic-hallucination model: trying to move against the paralysis worsens tightness and
pressure, and catastrophic thoughts ("I am dying") feed a fear loop that makes episodes longer. (S8, "Panic-Hallucination
(PH) Model".)

F55 [read] Case I: during a nap a man floated above his bed, heard "voices of family members outside his bedroom", and
saw the "ghost" of a recently deceased friend. When he turned his attention inward to a comforting thought, the
hallucinations "vanished". This is one case. (S8, "Case Examples".)

F57 [read] The university's release says people with it "typically perceive abrupt, loud noises—door slams, fireworks,

### False awakening

F58 [read] Celia Green's "Type 2" false awakening, as McCreery quotes it: "the subject appears to wake up in a realistic
manner, but to an atmosphere of suspense", becoming "aware of something uncanny in the atmosphere, and perhaps of
unwonted sounds and movements". (S10, PDF p.9, quoting Green 1968, p.121.)

F59 [read] McCreery's own subject described it: "the atmosphere grows tense and eerie". ~~Struggling made it worse.~~ *(second
reader: not in the source. The subject says the thought of being awake "made me panic and struggle to get free"; nothing
says struggling made it worse. Later: "realising that I need only relax in order to awake". S10, PDF p.9,
https://www.celiagreen.com/charlesmccreery/dreams-and-psychosis-online.pdf)* Lying
still, "everything would suddenly `click' back to normal". Moving an object during one and checking it later showed
the movements had not happened. (S10, PDF p.9, quoting McCreery 1973, p.118.)

F60 [summary] In a sleep lab, five sleep-paralysis episodes and two false awakenings were recorded. The EEG in false
awakening "followed the same profile as in sleep paralysis", mostly theta. From the theta in both, the authors conclude
that during sleep paralysis the brain "is not in an awake but in a dreaming state". (Mainieri et al. 2021, *J Clin
Sleep Med* 17:719-727, abstract.)

**Also read, outside the count.** *(second reader: this paragraph had no marks. The Wikipedia sentences are [summary];
the McCreery footnote is [read], confirmed at S10 PDF p.8.)* Wikipedia's "False awakening" (S16, Types) says Type 1 is the more common kind, and it
quotes Green with "unwanted [unusual] sounds" where McCreery's copy (F58) prints "unwonted". McCreery's footnote 5
(S10, PDF p.8) quotes Maury's 1861 guillotine dream through Mavromatis 1987: Maury woke "seized by the most violent
terror" as the rod of his bed fell on his neck.
