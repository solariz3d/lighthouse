## 2. Findings

### A. The surviving parent's own state, and how it reaches the child

F1. The review that summarises the field's changeable factors puts the surviving parent first: "Positive parenting by
the surviving parent is the single most consistently supported malleable mediator of the adjustment of parentally
bereaved children." [read] S1, PMC2888143, section "Facilitating a positive parent-child relationship".

F2. The strongest single test of the parenting path is mixed. Kwok et al. 2005 (214 children aged 7 to 16) found that
positive parenting mediated the link from parental distress to child problems in cross-sectional data, but "it was not
supported in the 3-wave longitudinal model"; positive parenting at wave 2 still predicted child problems 11 months
later. [summary: abstract, PMID 15901226]

F3. Melhem et al. 2008 (140 families bereaved by suicide, accident or sudden natural death; 99 control families):
bereaved offspring had a 3-fold risk of new depression (95% CI 1.3 to 7.0) after controlling for other risks. Higher
caregiver functioning was protective (OR 0.96, 95% CI 0.9 to 1.0). Caregiver and child symptoms correlated at
r = 0.26 to 0.48. [read] S5, PMC2654289, Abstract; "Predictors of outcome"; "Concomitant risk factors".
*(second reader: OK. The "offspring" were 7 to 25 years old, not only children (Abstract, "Participants"). The article
carries a 2019 correction for Suicidal Ideation Questionnaire coding that changed means in Tables 3 and 4 only and "did
not affect the conclusions", PMC6802050; none of the figures above is an SIQ mean.)*

F4. In that sample, caregivers in all three bereaved groups had similarly raised new-onset depression and PTSD against
control caregivers. The authors' clinical line: "The caregiver should be monitored for depression and PTSD, because
restoration of functioning is a positive prognosticator for offspring." [read] S5, "Concomitant risk factors" and
"Comment".

F5. Melhem et al. 2011 (182 children aged 7 to 18, sudden parental death, followed to 33 months) found three grief
paths: 58.8% low and falling, 30.8% high at 9 months then declining, and 10.4% high with "virtually no change" at 21
and 33 months. [read] S6, PMC4017669, Results 1a.

F6. The same study found "the combination of complicated grief in the surviving parent and in the child were
particularly potent in predicting incident depression in children up to three years after the death". [read] S6,
Discussion.

F7. Worden & Silverman 1993 (70 bereaved families with school-age children): 56% of the widowed parents had high
CES-D depression scores (above 16) at 4 months and 44% at one year. Higher depression went with more family life
changes, limited income, lack of peer support and more younger children. [summary: abstract via Crossref, S23]

F8. Before an expected death the well parent is often already struggling. Siegel et al. 1996 interviewed 103 well
spouses aged 55 or under of terminally ill cancer patients, with children 7 to 16 at home: 58% of men and 42% of women
scored in a clinically significant range for depressive distress. [summary: abstract, PMID 8788767]

F9. Shapiro, Howell & Kaplow 2014 (observed mother-child talks after the father's death): warm, sensitive, engaged
communication by mothers went with lower child maladaptive grief and depression, and "mothers who showed a blunted
emotional response to the loss, illustrated by unusually few depressive symptoms, were less effective at using these
strategies". [summary: abstract, PMID 24524545]

F10. Wardecker et al. 2017 (39 children): caregivers' positive emotion words while remembering the dead parent went
with lower child depression and anxiety only once about 103 days (depression) and 122 days (anxiety) had passed since
the death. The authors read this as children benefiting first from "observing “normative” levels of sadness or distress
in parental language and behavior in the first few months following the death". [read] S8, PMC5695886, Abstract and
Discussion.

F11. The FBP trial (156 families, 244 children 8 to 16, a 12-session group programme) at six years: fewer FBP parents
were above the cut point for moderate or higher depression (21.31% against 34.62%, OR 1.69), and FBP parents had
higher positive parenting. FBP youth had fewer externalizing problems and higher self-esteem; an externalizing disorder
was marginally less common (15.45% against 27.37%). [read] S2, PMC3835558, "Effects of the FBP on parents and
parent-child relationships" and "Effects of the FBP on child outcomes".

F12. In the FBP, the gains in child mental health at 11 months held for girls and for children with higher problems at
entry; boys in both the FBP and the self-study condition improved over time, with no difference between them.
[read] S2, "Effects of the FBP on child outcomes". *(second reader: the higher-problems effect is for children with
higher internalizing problems at entry, on parent-reported internalizing only; the girls' effect is on both
internalizing and externalizing, by caregiver and youth report. Same section.)*

F13. The FBP evidence comes mostly from illness deaths and mostly from mothers: cause of death 74% illness, 15%
accident, 11% homicide or suicide; caregivers 63% mothers, 21% fathers, 16% others. Families were excluded if the
caregiver met criteria for major depression. [read] S4, PMC11019585, "Participants".

F14. Hökkä & Aho 2026 (96 Finnish working-age widow(er)s, 89% women, mean age 48; 45% said the death came without prior
warning): the widows reported "shifting too much responsibility onto their children", difficulty setting boundaries,
feelings of inadequacy and worry for the children's coping, and that "the virtual world could become a substitute for
reality for their children"; among children's symptoms they listed guilt and self-blame for the death. [read] S16,
PMC13743714, "Participants" and "Increased Responsibility for the Children".

F15. Napier et al. 2026 (112 bereaved children 8 to 17; 59.6% had lost a parent) found three parenting patterns:
Passive 42.86%, Negative 34.82%, Positive 22.32%. The Positive group had fewer psychosocial difficulties (d = 0.86
against Passive, 1.67 against Negative) and higher resilience; there was no difference in PTSS or growth. Children in
the Passive group were more likely to have lost a parent. [read] S15, PMC12917021, Abstract and Results.

F16. Ellis et al. 2013 (33 UK adults bereaved before 18): even a temporary drop in the surviving parent's capacity
distressed children who did not understand it. The authors use Riches and Dawson's "double jeopardy": "the child not
only suffers the loss of a parent but the symbolic or temporary loss of other parent". [read] S9, PMC3569022,
Discussion.

### B. Open and closed communication

F17. Raveis, Siegel & Karus 1999 (83 families, a parent dead of cancer within 18 months): "The child's perception of the
surviving parent's level of openness in parental communication was found to be significantly correlated with lower
levels of depressive symptoms and state anxiety in bereaved children." [summary: abstract, Springer page for
doi 10.1023/A:1021697230387]

F18. Haine 2008 reports that "when children feel they must inhibit the expression of negative emotions they would like
to express, they are more likely to experience greater mental health problems". [read] S1, "Supporting adaptive
expression of emotion that the child wishes to express".

F19. In the review's account of the Grief Facilitation Inventory study (432 bereaved children and families), child
reports of caregivers' avoidance and inhibition of grief talk went with higher levels of all three domains of
maladaptive grief. [read] S3, PMC8794619, "Relations of Domains of Parenting to Children's Grief".

F20. The same account: "There was also an unexpected relation between encouraging an ongoing connection with the
deceased and higher levels of child maladaptive grief." The authors suggest children with worse grief may draw more of
it from the parent. [read] S3, same section.

F21. On silence about how the death happened: "Failure to communicate clearly about the circumstances of the death may
render children vulnerable to beliefs about possible culpability in causing or being unable to prevent the death".
[read] S3, "Circumstance-related Distress".

F22. Ellis 2013 describes the closed household: "In some families the deceased parent could not be as talked about at
all for fear that family members (particularly surviving parents) might not be able to cope with the emotions triggered
by a reminder"; such families became "individuals in a family". [read] S9, "Communication".

F23. In the same study an adult who was eight when his mother died, and who was told nothing about her illness,
described having had to "create a back story which probably had no foundation in reality at all". [read] S9,
"Communication".

F24. The same authors warn against a one-way rule: "communication is dialectic, dialogic and dynamic in nature", and they
advise exploring with each family what sharing grief means rather than simply promoting openness. [read] S9,
Discussion. (This stands against a simple reading of F17 to F19.)

F25. Weber et al. 2021 (Swedish families after a parent's death from cancer): parents rated family communication as
moderate and their children's health as good; adolescents' own reports showed low-quality communication and poor
psychological health. [summary: abstract, PMID 31256707]

F26. Bylund-Grenklo et al. 2015 (622 of 851 Swedish young adults who lost a parent to cancer at 13 to 16): 98% (595 of
610) said teenagers should be told when death is hours or days away; 59% had been told. A frequent reason for not
talking: "one (n=106) or both (n=25) of the parents together with the teenage child had pretended that the illness was
not that serious". "Up to a couple of hours before the loss, 43% of participants had not realized that death was
imminent." [summary: abstract, PMID 25467964]

F27. Silverman on talking with bereaved children: "it is easier for children to talk with you if you ask them who
died, rather than start a discussion with how they feel about their loss". [read] S18, Psychology Today, "How do ideas
get generated" (2009), paragraph beginning "One thing that surprised everyone".

F28. Silverman on what bereaved teenagers say they need: the parent's attention, to know the family's financial
situation, and "They need to know that their wish to be quiet is respected." [read] S19, Psychology Today, "How
teenagers cope" (2009), paragraph beginning "What do teenagers say about this?".

### C. Mutual protection and silence

F29. Bylund-Grenklo et al. 2021 (the same 622) asked about the first six months with the item “I withheld my grief to
protect my other parent.” The abstract gives 24% agreeing and the results text 25%. Agreeing was associated with
long-term unresolved grief, OR 1.83 (95% CI 1.23 to 2.73). [read] S10, PMC8161967, "Measurements", Abstract and
"Prevalence of the different acute grief experiences and reactions". *(second reader: "agreeing" means moderately or
completely agree, 97 + 53 = 150 of 615 = 24.4%, Table 2; a further 199 of 615 (32%) answered "slightly agree", so 349
of 615 (57%) agreed to some degree. [read] PMC8161967, Table 2, row "CONCEALED GRIEF". See section 6.)*

F30. In the same survey 57% did not have a way to grieve that felt okay in the first six months (OR 4.32 for long-term
unresolved grief), and 42% agreed with “I clenched my teeth, built a wall around me and lived on as if nothing had
happened.” Being male (OR 1.77), good family cohesion (OR 2.17) and having talked with the dying parent about what was
important (OR 2.00) predicted an okay way to grieve. [read] S10, Abstract, "Measurements", Results.

F31. Haine 2008 names the child's side of mutual protection: "parentally bereaved children sometimes feel it is their
job to take care of a grieving parent and make them feel less sad". The FBP told children this is not their job and
asked parents to find “adult ears” for their own problems. [read] S1, "Increasing child adaptive control beliefs".

F32. Scott et al. 2025 (nine Scottish young adults ~~bereaved as teenagers or young adults~~ *(second reader: bereaved
from age 10 to 21; PMC12291619, Results, first paragraph: "they experienced parental loss at a mean age of 16.7 years
(range 10–21)"; seven deaths from cancer, one heart attack, one suicide)*): all participants from
two-parent families described "taking on responsibility for their surviving parent’s wellbeing and loneliness"; one
described supporting the surviving parent with money. [read] S12, PMC12291619, "Cumulative stressors".

F33. A single consented case (a 14-year-old girl whose father died after a long illness; the family rarely discussed
it): "The girl described a need to appear strong for her mother, suppressing her own sadness to avoid adding more
pain." She also reported difficulty sleeping. [read] S13, PMC12782988, "Case illustration #2".

F34. The parent's side of the same silence, in a widow's words: "how much I have contributed to their grief by not
daring to help them express their feelings". [read] S16, "Increased Responsibility for the Children".

F35. The FBP taught a middle course: "Parents were taught that while they did not need to hide their distress from
their children, they should try to reassure their child" that the family would get through it. [read] S1, "Reducing
parental distress".

### D. Fear of losing the surviving parent; routines; sleep and dreams

F36. O'Hara et al. 2024 measured fear of abandonment with items such as “How much do you worry that you may be left all
alone?” and describe it as the most robust self-system belief linking risk and protective factors to bereaved youths'
problems and grief. FBP-driven drops in stressful events predicted lower fear of abandonment; for girls, fear of
abandonment predicted maladaptive grief and teacher-reported internalizing six years later. [read] S4, Background,
"Fear of Abandonment (Youth Report)", Abstract.

F37. The review's account of Wolchik et al. 2009: their positive parenting construct included positive dyadic routines,
stable positive events in the family, the child feeling understood and open communication; its link to maladaptive
grief ran through a lower fear that no one would care for the child in future. [read] S3, "Relations of Domains of
Parenting to Children's Grief".

F38. The FBP's routine tools: a weekly, active "Family Fun Time" presented as a break from grief; joint book-reading as
a cheap positive routine; holiday guidance (simplify, keep some old traditions, add new ones). [read] S1, "Increasing
positive family interactions", "Supporting adaptive expression", "Reducing child exposure to negative life events".

F39. Haine 2008: crying and sleep disturbances are common soon after the death and decrease over time (citing Silverman
& Worden 1992; 1993). [read] S1, "Supporting adaptive expression of emotion".

F40. Bylund-Grenklo et al. 2016 (the same Swedish sample, 6 to 9 years on): 49% reported no or little grief resolution.
Against those with resolved grief, insomnia risk was raised (RR 2.3 for sons, 1.7 for daughters), as were fatigue and
moderate to severe depression. [summary: abstract, PMID 26899858]

F41. The FBP taught families that "it is not unusual for children to think they see their parent who has died or to
dream about the deceased parent". [read] S1, introduction, on education about the grief process.

F42. Silverman, Nickman & Worden 1992: during the year after the death, children built an inner construction of the
dead parent, and this altered, continued relationship appeared to help them cope. [summary: abstract, PMID 1443058]

F43. Karydi 2018 (135 adults bereaved in childhood): the surviving parent's role in facilitating grief promoted a
positive continuing bond and adult functioning; the bond itself had only a weak association with better functioning.
[summary: abstract, PMID 28816620]

### E. Anticipated death after illness against sudden death

F44. Saldinger et al. 1999: "forewarning of death is not associated with more favorable mental health outcomes than is
sudden death of a parent". [summary: abstract, PMID 9990435]

F45. Saldinger et al. 2003 (58 bereaved children and their surviving parents) describe the child's exposure to the
dying parent's decline as traumatic stress, plus secondary stress from "the stress of watching other loved ones in the
family succumb to terror and anxiety about the impending death". [summary: abstract, PMID 12868295]

F46. Saldinger et al. 2004 (41 bereaved spouses): mothers were more child-centred than fathers, and "parents surviving
sudden deaths more child-centered than those surviving anticipated deaths"; "Lengthy illness was associated with less
child-centered parenting." More child-centred parenting went with fewer child symptoms. [summary: abstract,
PMID 15801376]

F47. Kaplow et al. 2014 (63 children, 38 caregivers): children after a prolonged illness showed higher maladaptive
grief (d = 3.13) and PTSS (d = 3.33) than after a sudden natural death; caregivers did not differ in grief or PTSS, but
those bereaved by sudden natural death reported more depression (d = 1.36). Child and caregiver reports of the cause
differed, most in suicides. [summary: abstract, PMID 24478197]

F48. Sheehan et al. 2016 (26 hospice families; 30 adolescents): adolescents managed a "well world" and an "ill world"
in five stages, from keeping them separate to returning changed. The introduction notes children "are likely to show
the greatest distress just prior to the parent’s death". [read] S17, PMC12096904, Results and Introduction.

F49. Haine 2008 on type of death: studies of "cause and type (sudden vs. expected)" indicate that "cause of death alone
is not a major predictor of mental health problems". [read] S1, "Cause and type of death".

F50. Within sudden deaths, Melhem 2008 found the type of death (suicide, accident, sudden natural) did not predict new
depression in the bereaved group. [read] S5, "Predictors of outcome and independent contribution of bereavement".

F51. Melhem 2011 found that loss by accident helped predict membership of the two higher-grief groups. [read] S6,
Results 1b.

F52. Berg, Rostila & Hjern 2016 (Swedish cohort, 862,554 people born 1973 to 1982): a parent's natural death raised the
risk of outpatient care for depression slightly (maternal death: HR 1.19 men, 1.15 women). "Death from external causes
consistently had higher effect size compared with natural deaths", up to HR 3.23 for men's hospital admission after a
mother's death. Loss at preschool age carried more risk than loss as a teenager. [summary: abstract, PMID 27058980]

F53. Pham et al. 2018 (216 youths bereaved by suicide, accident or sudden natural death; 172 controls; up to 7 years):
the peak incidence of depression was in the first 2 years, mainly in those who lost a parent at 12 or younger.
[summary: abstract, PMID 29921145]

F54. In older adults (Carr et al. 2001; 210 widowed people aged 65 and over), forewarning did not change depression,
anger, shock or overall grief; prolonged forewarning raised anxiety at 6 and 18 months; sudden death raised intrusive
thoughts at 6 months only. "The findings call into question the widespread belief that grief is more severe if death
is sudden". [summary: abstract, PMID 11445616]

F55. In sudden deaths (Melhem 2008), "A recollection of a supportive conversation with the deceased was associated with
a higher risk of depression" (OR 4.7, 95% CI 1.8 to 12.1). [read] S5, "Predictors of outcome" and "Comment".
*(second reader: OK. The Results name the predictor "having a last confiding conversation with the deceased", and the
Comment reads it as "consistent with other studies finding that the closer the relationship to the deceased, the higher
the risk of depression". PMC2654289, "Predictors of outcome" and "Comment".)*

### F. Course, prevalence and context

F56. Worden & Silverman 1996 (Harvard Child Bereavement Study, assessed at 4 months, 1 year and 2 years): most
differences from matched controls were not obvious until two years; 19% at one year and 21% at two years showed
serious problems. [summary: abstract via Crossref, S22]

F57. The ERIC Digest's account of Worden 1996: 125 children aged 6 to 17, 74% of whom had lost a father; 13% still
cried daily or weekly after a year; children became anxious over the safety of other loved ones or themselves; "Many
children in this study expressed guilt about remembered misbehavior or missed opportunities to express affection".
[summary: ERIC Digest ED475393, a secondary account of Worden 1996]

F58. Dowdney 2000 (review): about one child in five is likely to develop a psychiatric disorder, and "The highest rates
of reported difficulties are found in boys." [summary: abstract, PMID 11079425]

F59. Sgier et al. 2026 found 13 of 75 Disney and Pixar films (17.3%) showing the death of one or both parents, and:
"It is surprisingly rare for a surviving parent to be portrayed as a protector and supporter of their child."
[read] S14, PMC13433893, "Film Selection" and "Finding Protection and Relationships".

F60. Melhem & Brent close their 2016 commentary with Freud: "We find a place for what we lose. Although we know that
after such a loss the acute stage of mourning will subside, we also know that a part of us shall remain inconsolable".
[read] S7, PMC5119926, last paragraph before Acknowledgments.
