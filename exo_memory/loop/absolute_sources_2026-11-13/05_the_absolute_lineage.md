### Bentov's own word

**F13.** [read] Bentov divides creation in two: "we can divide creation into two components, the absolute and the
relative. The absolute is fixed, eternal, and invisible, while the relative is the visible, manifest, and changing
aspect." (S5, 1977 scan, pp. 65-66; the same words in both 1979 scans).

**F14.** [read] He writes the word in lower case in prose, once in quotation marks: the "absolute" "contains all
consciousness there is in the universe" (S5, 1977 scan). *(second reader: the words are found in all three scans; the
1977 OCR hyphenates "uni- verse". "Once" and "on that spread" cannot be established from ~100-character search fragments:
UNSUPPORTED. The figure's fragment in the 1977 scan sits beside the running head "60 STALKING THE WILD PENDULUM", while
F13's sentence is on pp. 65-66.)* A figure on that spread labels "ABSOLUTE: The sum total of
consciousness in the universe" and "RELATIVE REALITIES: All reality bands below the ABSOLUTE" (S5, chapter "Quantity and
Quality of Consciousness"). The memo capitalises the word throughout (S1, S2).

**F15.** [read] He ties the absolute to the pendulum's rest: "the state of the absolute is a state of the highest
potential energy. It is a state of rest of the pendulum." (S5, 1977 scan). Bentov's pendulum, at its point of rest, "has
to become nonmaterial" for a very short time and expand into space (S5, 1979 scans; the 1977 scan matches up to "for a
very short period of time and"). *(second reader: the 1977 scan also has the rest: "and expand into space at an almost
infinite velocity" (Open Library search-inside, item `stalkingwildpend0000bent`).)* The memo's click-out diagram is credited to this book (F5).

**F16.** [read] He sets the observer at the rest point. Something goes out at "infinite speeds into space at the point
of rest of the pendulum or body", and he calls this "observer" "the nonphysical 'us,' our psyche" (S5, 1977 scan).

**F17.** [read] His account of creation starts with a separation: "consciousness/intelligence is separating itself from
the continuum so that it can start acting. It has thus become an individualized entity." (S5, 1977 scan).

**F18.** [read] He then gives the Creator a body: "the Creator has to settle down and start to draw up plans of what to
do within His real estate, which is His body." (S5, all three scans).

**F19.** [read] ~~He opens the pendulum with a household clock:~~ *(second reader: UNSUPPORTED. The passage begins "Let us
go back to the old grandfather clock", under a running head, in all three scans, so the clock is a return to an image
used earlier, not the opening. Where it first appears was not found.)* "the old grandfather clock with its leisurely
swinging pendulum" (S5, 1979 scans; the 1977 scan hyphenates "lei- surely").

**F20.** [read] The "cosmic egg" and the "white hole" of memo ¶24 ~~are Bentov's own~~ *(second reader: WRONG as worded.

**F21.** [read] Bentov was born Emerich Tobiás in Humenné, Czechoslovakia, on 9 August 1923. He invented a steerable
cardiac catheter and died on 25 May 1979 in the crash of American Airlines Flight 191 (S6, lead and infobox).

### Ein Sof and tzimtzum: "Hebrew mystical philosophy"

**F22.** [read] Ein Sof is Hebrew for "(There is) no end", ~~and is usually translated "infinite"~~ *(second reader:
"usually" is not in the source. The lead gives meaning "infinite", literally "(There is) no end", and says it "may be
translated as 'unending', '(there is) no end', or infinity"; it also says the term was probably derived from Solomon ibn
Gabirol's "the Endless One". S8, wikitext lines 4-5.)*. Wikipedia says it was
first used by Azriel of Gerona (c. 1160-1238), who held a Neoplatonic view "emphasized by the negation of any attribute"
(S8, "Ein Sof", lead).

**F23.** [read] Luria, in Vital's record, opens with a light that leaves no room: "before the emanations were emanated
and the creations were created, there was a simple supernal light that filled all of existence, and there was no empty
space" (S7, *Etz Chaim* 1:2:2, English CC0). Hebrew: ולא היה שום מקום פנוי. Then the Ein Sof "contracted Himself" (צמצם
את עצמו) at the middle point. The light "withdrew to the sides surrounding the central point, and then a void, air, and
empty space were left". The void was "perfectly circular" (S7, 1:2:2).

**F24.** [read] The contraction has a stated purpose: it "is meant to reveal the root of judgment in order to give the
measure of judgment afterwards in the worlds" (S7, 1:2:3; Hebrew לגלות שורש הדינין).

**F25.** [read] Wikipedia lists Luria's four themes: tzimtzum, the shattering of the vessels (*shevirat ha-kelim*),
repair (*tikkun*), and the divine personas (*partzufim*). It says tzimtzum "is connected to the concept of exile" (S8,
"Tzimtzum", "Lurianic interpretation").

**F26.** [read] Wikipedia's list of the sefirot under Ein Sof pairs "Chesed or Gedulah (Loving Kindness or Mercy)" with
"Gevurah or Din (Power or Judgement)" (S8, "Ein Sof", "The Ten Sefirot").

**F27.** [inferred] The memo's "Absolute at rest in infinity" (F9) matches Ein Sof on one point: no end means infinity.
It differs on another. Luria's Ein Sof before creation is a light filling all, and creation starts with a withdrawal
that leaves a void (F23). Bentov's starts with consciousness separating from a continuum (F17). Both start with a
separation.

### Nirguna Brahman in Advaita

**F28.** [read] IEP: "This absolute Brahman is known as nirguņa Brahman, or Brahman 'without qualities'". It can be met
"as a personal God, known as saguņa Brahman, or Brahman with qualities", usually called īśvara (S9, §2a).

**F29.** [read] Müller's 1884 rendering of *neti neti*, Brihadaranyaka II.3.6: "Next follows the teaching (of Brahman)

means 'not (this), not (this)'"; it "denies any nameability of brahman" (S10, §4.2).

**F30.** [read] SEP likens Śaṅkara's approach to "a dream character attempting to discover the waking ground of their

### Schelling, Hegel, Bradley

**F33.** [read] Schelling, quoted in SEP from SW I/4, p. 403: "For most people see in the essence of the Absolute
nothing but pure night", and "I want to show here (...) how that night of the Absolute can be turned into day for
knowledge" (S12, §3).

**F34.** [read] The sources disagree about Schelling. SEP reports Hegel's reading, that Schelling's absolute is the
"'night in which all cows are black'", and answers: "This is not a valid interpretation of Schelling's argument." (S12,
§3). Hegel's own words: the Absolute "as the night in which, as we say, all cows are black – that is the very naïveté of
emptiness of knowledge" (S13, §16). *(second reader: the words are in S13, but Baillie's 1910 text ends the clause
"naivete of vacuous knowledge" (Archive `cu31924097557171`); see section 6.)*

**F35.** [read] In the 1809 freedom essay, as SEP quotes it (SW I/7, p. 358), the real is "in God" but is "only the
ground of His existence, it is nature in God; an essence which is inseparable from God, but different from Him" (S12,
§4).

**F36.** [read] SEP: Schelling posits "an unconscious, dark principle and a conscious principle". He pairs a contractive
"dark force", which he sometimes calls gravity, with an expansive "light". If either ruled alone, "there would not be a
world" (S12, §4).

**F37.** [read] Schelling, as SEP quotes him (SW I/7, p. 399): "veil of melancholy which is spread over the whole of
nature, the deep indestructible melancholy of all life". Bowie gives its source: "everything finite must 'go to ground'
and that we are aware of this" (S12, §4).

**F38.** [read] Hegel: "The truth is the whole." "Of the Absolute it must be said that it is essentially a result, that
only at the end is it what it is in very truth" (S13, §20).

**F39.** [read] Hegel: the life of God may be called "love disporting with itself; but this idea falls into edification,
and even sinks into insipidity, if it lacks the seriousness, the suffering, the patience, and the labour of the
negative" (S13, §19).

**F40.** [read] Readers disagree on whether Hegel's Absolute is God. SEP gives the "traditional metaphysical view": "a
metaphysico-religious view of God qua Absolute Spirit". It sets that against a post-Kantian, "non-metaphysical" reading
(S14, §§2.2-2.3).

**F41.** [read] Bradley: "The Absolute has no history of its own, though it contains histories without number." (S15,
ch. XXVI, p. 499). On the next page: "The Absolute has no seasons, but all at once bears its leaves, fruit, and
blossoms." (S15, p. 500). Bradley's sentence that the Absolute "enters into, but is itself incapable of, evolution and
progress" is on p. 499 of S15. SEP says A. J. Ayer ridiculed it and gives it as "p. 442" (S16, §2). The two editions
paginate differently, so both numbers are given here. *(second reader: "The two editions paginate differently" is
[inferred], not [read]. SEP does not name its edition in what was read, so p. 442 may be another edition or an error.
In S15, Bradley's subject is "it", this one Reality: "And it enters into, but is itself incapable of, evolution and
progress" (p. 499); "the Absolute enters into" is Ayer's paraphrase, as SEP says.)*

**F42.** [read] SEP on Bradley: "the Absolute must comprehend both evil and good". It "is in such a way further from
evil than from good, but is itself neither"; "a Supreme Being, but not a personal God" (S16, §6).

### The word itself, and Cusa

**F49.** [read] Lewis & Short, *absolvo*: "to loosen from, to make loose, set free, detach, untie", with the figure
"derived from fetters". Sense D: "to acquit, declare innocent". Sense E: "to bring a work to a close, to complete,
finish", the figure "prob. derived from detaching a finished web from the loom" (S21).

**F50.** [read] Lewis & Short, *absolutus*, quotes Cicero (*Fin.* 2, 27, 87): "nec appellatur vita beata nisi confecta
atque absoluta", glossed "when not completed and concluded" (S21). My translation: "nor is a life called happy unless it
is completed and concluded."

**F51.** [read] Lewis & Short gives the grammarians' "Nomen absolutum, which gives a complete sense without any thing
annexed, e. g.: 'deus'" (Priscian) (S21).

**F52.** [read] Wiktionary: English "absolute" was first attested around 1380. It comes from Latin *absolūtus*
"unconditional; unfettered; completed", the participle of *absolvō* "loosen, set free, complete", from *ab* "away" and
*solvo* "to loose" (S22, Etymology 1 *(second reader: the English section has one heading, "Etymology", unnumbered)*).

**F53.** [read] SEP on Cusa's *On Learned Ignorance* (1440): "God is the absolute Maximum; the universe is a created
image of God, the 'contracted' or restricted maximum". And: "'Absolute' is used in its etymological sense of 'free from'
(ab-solutus)" (S20, §2.1).

**F54.** [summary] Wikipedia, citing Inwood (1992, p. 27): the philosophical term "first occurs in the work of Nicholas
of Cusa". Hegel's use "was developed in response to" Schelling's (S23, "G. W. F. Hegel"). Inwood was not opened.

### Čapek, *The Absolute at Large*

**F55.** [read] The Czech title is *Továrna na absolutno*, which Wikipedia renders "The Factory for the Absolute".
Wikipedia dates the novel to 1922 and links Šárka B. Hrbková's English translation (S25). The 1927 Macmillan edition
reads "Published May, 1927" (S24, front matter).
