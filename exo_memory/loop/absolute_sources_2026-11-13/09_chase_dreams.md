## 2. Findings

### How common the chase dream is, and at what ages

F1. Being chased without injury was the most prevalent of 55 typical themes among 1,181 first-year students: "the two
most prevalent themes were 1-being chased or pursued, but not physically injured (81.5%) and 32-sexual experiences
(76.5%)". [read] S1, p.215.

F2. Within that student sample, age made no difference to the chase theme: 82.8% at 18 or under against 80.8% at 19 or
over, p = .400. [read] S1, Table 1, p.217.

F3. Chase was also the theme most often named as the earliest remembered: "Item 1, Chase/pursuit without injury was both
most prevalent (81.5%) and most often selected as the earliest (21.6%)." [read] S1, Fig. 1 caption, p.216.

F4. Of 888 students who dated their earliest typical theme (any theme), 13.3% put it at age 4 or younger, 32.5% at 5-6,
26.8% at 7-8 and 16.6% at 9-10. [read] S1, Fig. 2 caption, p.220.

F5. The authors note that the earliest themes (chase, trying again and again, flying, falling) share movement: "vigorous
motor activity or movement is clearly central to the theme." [read] S1, p.231.

F6. Other typical themes in the same table: "trying again and again to do something" 53.5%, "vividly sensing . . . a
presence in the room" 48.3%, "discovering a new room at home" 32.3%. [read] S1, Table 1, pp.217-218.

F7. Dreams of the dead are typical too: "a person now alive as dead" 54.1%, "a person now dead as alive" 38.4%; these were
rated as personally important as often as the most prevalent themes. [read] S1, Table 1 and p.232.

F8. A 2026 replication gave the same questionnaire to 30,686 people aged 10-85 and found that the number of typical themes
reported falls with age; theme rankings correlated strongly across groups. [summary: abstract] S2.

F9. Questionnaires and diaries disagree: "themes of falling and of being chased are among the most frequently reported
themes in studies based on questionnaire or interview data while appearing much less frequently in prospective logs".
[read] S4, Introduction.

### Chase in nightmares and recurrent dreams: child against adult

F10. In 1,216 most-recent nightmares (189 from childhood or adolescence), "Childhood nightmares more often included being
chased, evil presence, and falling, whereas the nightmares that occurred in adulthood more often included interpersonal
conflicts and failure/helplessness." [read] S3, Results, "Factors Affecting Nightmare Themes".

F11. The same paper reports, from Zadra (1996), that being chased appeared in 41.5% of childhood recurrent dreams against
14.6% of adults'. Zadra's chapter itself was not opened. [read] S3, Introduction.

F12. ~~Of 126 chase nightmares, the chaser was~~ *(second reader: the 126 is Table 4's n; the percentages exclude "the 44
cases with no information about the aggressor", so they are of the classified cases only. 126 minus 44 is 82 by the text;
the three figures fit a base of 84, e.g. 50/84 = 59.52% [inferred arithmetic]. S3, PMC5837849, "Perpetrators in
Nightmares of Being Chased". The same paragraph: of human chasers whose sex was known, "91.67% were male (44 out of 48)".
These nightmares are of all ages, not childhood only.)* Among chase nightmares whose chaser could be classified, it was
human in 59.52%, an animal in 19.05%, and a creature or thing in 21.43%
("monsters, ghosts, and devils and also objects such as a giant sphere"). [read] S3, Results, "Perpetrators".

F13. Revonsuo reports from Domhoff (1996) and Hartmann (1984) that lifelong nightmares most often feature being chased or
attacked: "Such dreams usually begin in childhood and involve being chased by a monster or a wild animal." In adults the
chaser was more often a large unfamiliar man or a gang. [read] S5, ~~p.885~~ *(second reader: p.886 by the page's own
printed number, PDF page 10 of the copy named in the S5 row of section 1)*.

F14. Revonsuo reports Robbins & Houshi (1983): 60% of 123 students had recurrent dreams, many from childhood, and the one
frequent type was being threatened or pursued, with the dreamer watching, hiding or running away. [read] S5, ~~p.885~~
*(second reader: p.886, section 3.4.2.3)*.

F15. Revonsuo reports Hall (1955), 106 dreams of being attacked: "The dreamer usually reacted to the attack by running,
escaping, or hiding (unless she woke up)." [read] S5, ~~p.883~~ *(second reader: p.884, PDF page 8)*.

F16. In diaries from 331 adults, nightmares were more likely than bad dreams to contain physical aggression, being chased,
evil forces and accidents, and more often had an unfortunate ending. [read] S4, Results and Discussion.

### Waking at the peak

F17. ~~The diagnostic definition used:~~ *(second reader: this is the study's own working definition, introduced with
"Consistent with previous work, 15 , 19 nightmares were defined as", S4, PMC3900621, Methods; it is not the diagnostic
manuals' wording. S3 quotes ICSD-3 and DSM-5 as defining nightmares as "repeated occurrences of extended, extremely

no waking clause in that sentence; S3, PMC5837849, Introduction.)* The study's working definition: nightmares are "very
disturbing dreams in which the unpleasant visual imagery and/or
emotions cause the person to wake up"; bad dreams do not wake the sleeper. [read] S4, Methods.

F18. Where dreamers named what woke them (36.5% of 253 nightmares), the reasons were an immediate threat 42.4%, the
emotion's intensity 25%, a salient element 18.5%, and waking on purpose 14.1%. [read] S4, Results.

### Threat simulation theory (Revonsuo 2000) and its critics

F19. The theory: "the biological function of dreaming is to simulate threatening events, and to rehearse threat perception
and threat avoidance." [read] S5, abstract, p.877.

F20. Revonsuo's evidence from children: animal characters are about 25-30% of characters at ages 2-6, about 15% at 7-12
and about 5% in adults (from Domhoff 1996); in Van de Castle's 741 reports from ages 4-16, the share of animal dreams
falls from 39.4% at ages 4-6 to 13.7% in the oldest two-year group. [read] S5, ~~p.884~~ *(second reader: p.885, PDF
page 9, section 3.4.2.2)*.

F21. Revonsuo grants the dispute over young children's dreams: laboratory REM sampling (Foulkes) finds dreaming
"rudimentary" before about 5-9, home reports do not; he cites an estimate that "10–50% of children at the age of 3–5 so
frequently have nightmares as to alarm their parents". [read] S5, note 5, ~~p.898~~ *(second reader: p.899, PDF page 23;
the estimate is credited there to "The American Sleep Disorders Association (1990)")*.

F22. A home study of 349 dreams from 40 children aged 3.8-8.7 found 70% of characters human and 7% animal, against the
laboratory picture of animal-filled, static preschool dreams. [read] S7, abstract and Results.

F23. Severely traumatised Kurdish children reported more dreams and more, and more severe, dream threats than less
traumatised or Finnish children; read as support for the theory. [summary: abstract] S6, Valli et al. 2005.

F24. A high-crime sample (South Africa, n = 208) reported fewer threat dreams than a low-crime one (Wales, n = 116);
realistic survival threats were in under 20% of dreams and escape in under 2%. [summary: abstract] S6, Malcolm-Smith 2008.
*(second reader: both figures are shares of all dreams: "Escape from dream threats occurred in less than 2% of dreams"
(PMID 17702604 abstract). The same group's 2012 abstract restates it per threat dream: "successful threat avoidance
occurring in approximately one-fifth of such dreams" (PMID 22196966). So under 2% does not mean that under 2% of chased
dreamers escaped. [summary: abstract])*

F25. In 212 recurrent dreams, 66% held threats, but under 15% showed realistic survival situations, and the dreamer
"rarely succeeded in fleeing the threat despite important and appropriate efforts"; "mixed support". [summary: abstract]
S6, Zadra, Desjardins & Marcotte 2006.

### Bad dreams and nightmares in children

F26. In 987 Quebec children followed from 29 months to 6 years, mothers rated bad dreams "sometimes" for 65.2-69.3% at
each age, "often" for 1.3-3.9% and "always" for 0.0-0.8%. [read] S8, Results, "Prevalence of Bad Dreams".

F27. Bad dreams were persistent: of children with them at one age, more than 80% still had them the next year. [read] S8,
Discussion.

F28. Of 190 children aged 4-12, 80.5% reported scary dreams; they rose from 4-6 to 7-9 and fell at 10-12, and scary dreams
about imaginary creatures fell with age. [summary: abstract] S9, Muris 2000.

F29. A review says nightmare frequency "may peak around the age of 10", with girls reporting more from 10 to 15.
[summary: abstract] S9, Gauchat 2014.

### Grief and nightmares

F30. Revonsuo reports Feldman & Hersen (1967): frequent recurring nightmares in adults were related to waking concern
about death and to the death of a close relative or friend before age 10. The 1967 paper was not opened. [read] S5,
~~p.885~~ *(second reader: p.886, section 3.4.2.3)*.

F31. In Nielsen's nightmare group, 53.2% (33/62) believed an event preceded their onset but only 35.5% (22/62) could
describe one; in both nightmare and control groups the commonest named events involved parents: "death of a parent or
other family member, parental divorce or separation, family discord, and bullying". [read] S10, Results.

### Imagery rehearsal therapy (Krakow)

F32. The AASM's definition: "IRT is a modified cognitive behavioral therapy technique which involves altering the content
of a nightmare by creating a new set of positive images and rehearsing the rewritten dream scenario for 10–20 minutes per
day while awake." [read] S11, "Image Rehearsal Therapy (IRT)".

F33. IRT is the one therapy the AASM recommends for both PTSD-associated nightmares and nightmare disorder; the paper puts
nightmare disorder at 4% of US adults. [read] S11, Position Statements and Introduction.

F34. Krakow's three steps, all awake: select a nightmare, "change the nightmare any way you wish", rehearse the new version
5-20 min a day. In girls aged 13-18 (9 treated, 10 controls) nightmares per month fell 71% at 3 months. [summary: abstract]
S12, J Adolesc Health 2001.

F35. Domhoff's plain account: nightmares "can be reduced by writing down a new ending to the nightmare of one's own
choosing, and then imagining that new ending several times each day". [read] S14, Chapter 1.

F36. In a 2023 IRT case, the rewrite "could be outlandish, introduce rescuers, invoke her Super Hero superpowers"; the
patient was told to give the dream a positive ending. [read] S13, "Session four".

F37. Rewriting is not the only thing that works: rescripting and plain imaginal exposure (no rewrite) both cut nightmare
frequency against a wait-list by similar amounts (d 0.74 and 0.70; n = 104). [summary: abstract] S15, Kunze 2017.

F38. The two seem to work differently: rescripting through mastery of the nightmare, exposure through tolerating its
negative emotions. [summary: abstract] S15, Kunze 2019.

F39. One session of rescripting, exposure, or positive imagery ~~unrelated to the nightmare~~ *(second reader: the
abstract says only "positive imagery as an active control"; "unrelated to the nightmare" is not in it, PMID 33477151.
Each arm was one session "and 4 weeks of practice at home"; the d values are each group's change, not differences
between groups.)* all reduced distress (d -1.04,
-0.68, -0.57; n = 96), with no differences between them. [summary: abstract] S15, Schmid 2021.

F40. A systematic review found no consensus on how nightmare treatments work; mastery was the most cited hypothesis.
[summary: abstract] S15, Rousseau & Belleville 2018.

F41. Not every trial is positive: six group sessions of IRT did not beat an active comparison in 124 Vietnam veterans with
chronic PTSD. [summary: abstract] S15, Cook 2010.

F42. In children aged 9-11 with weekly nightmares (n = 20), IRT reduced frequency against a wait-list, held at 9 months.
[summary: abstract] S15, St-Onge 2009.

F43. In children aged 6-11 (10 child-mother pairs), drawing changed versions of the nightmare for a month added a fall in
distress and anxiety, but not in frequency. [summary: abstract] S15, Simard & Nielsen 2009.

F44. The older "Senoi" claim, as paraphrased by Domhoff, was "Always confront and conquer danger in dreams"; his verdict:
"Stewart's ideas about dream control do not work." [read] S14, Chapter 1.

### Lucid dreaming and nightmares

F45. Of 1,216 nightmares, 39 were lucid: "In most of the lucid dreams (n = 20), the dreamer described that she or he still
experienced fear or another negative emotion and was not able to change the dream action." [read] S3, Results.

F46. In the same set, 9 lucid dreamers used lucidity to wake themselves and only 10 lucid dreams reduced fear or changed
the course. [read] S3, Results.

F47. In a pilot trial of lucid dreaming therapy (23 nightmare sufferers), "Lucidity was not necessary for a reduction in
nightmare frequency." [summary: abstract] S16, Spoormaker 2006.

F48. A 2023 systematic review found 4 trials, 2 case series and 5 case reports of lucid dreaming therapy, and none in
children. [summary: abstract] S16, Ouchene 2023.

F49. In students aged 6-19, lucid dreaming was "quite pronounced in young children" and its incidence dropped at about
16. [summary: abstract] S16, Voss 2012.

### Text and clocks inside dreams

F50. In 456 dream reports, two blind scorers found "no instances of reading, no instances of writing, and one instance of
probable calculating". [read] S17, abstract, p.103.

F51. The count excluded short signs: scorers were told not to count "simply seeing a sign with one or two words"; the
example given was No Exit. [read] S17, p.106.

F52. Hartmann reports Hacker (1911): when he tried to focus on dream text "he remembered seeing a blank page, or material
that looked like writing, though he could not distinguish actual letters or words". [read] S17, p.105.

F53. Piller quotes Fox (1962): "The print seems clear enough until one tries to read it: then the letters become blurred
or run together, or fade away, or change to others". [read] S18, p.275.

F54. Piller reports Worsley (1988), one lucid dreamer: single words read 9 of 10 times, two words 8 of 10, short sentences
2 of 7, long sentences 0 of 5. [read] S18, p.275.

F55. Piller quotes LaBerge & DeGracia (2000): "text, upon rereading, can change in either form, lexical structure,
semantic structure, or based on rhyme and alliteration". [read] S18, p.275.

F56. The Lucidity Institute FAQ says: "Research shows that text in dreams changes 75% of the time it is re-read once and
changes 95% it is re-read twice." It names no study. [read] S21, section 3.2.

F57. The same FAQ tells readers to "Carry some text with you or wear a digital watch" and to look away and back to see if
the characters change; no data on clocks is given. [read] S21, section 3.2.

F58. A peer-reviewed paper repeats the text claim with a book as its source: "written text tends to change upon second
inspection in dreams" (LaBerge & Rheingold 1991). [read] S19, p.208.

F59. Reading and writing being rare in dreams was replicated with diaries; whether the cause is REM brain chemistry or a
less active prefrontal cortex "remains an open question". [summary: abstract] S23.

F60. ~~Felt time is roughly kept:~~ *(second reader: the paper does not measure felt time. It times dream actions in real
time by eye signals. Its own result: "Longer durations in the dream state were present for all three tasks, but
significant differences were found only for the tasks with motor activity (walking and gymnastics)", S22, PMC3893623,
abstract. Counting also ran longer in this study, but not significantly.)* Counting in lucid dreams took the same time as
awake (LaBerge 1985), but squats took about
40% longer, and walking and a gymnastics routine also ran longer. [read] S22, abstract and Introduction.
