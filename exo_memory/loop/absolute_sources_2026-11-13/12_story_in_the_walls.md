## 2. Findings

### Jenkins (S1, S2): the four kinds, in his words

F1. [read] Jenkins: ~~"Environmental storytelling creates the preconditions for an immersive narrative experience in at least
one of four ways":~~ *(second reader: rule slip, two adjacent quotations from one sentence came to 50 words; first half now
paraphrased: environmental storytelling sets up an immersive narrative in at least one of four ways. Words found in S1, 4th
paragraph of the section.)* "spatial stories can evoke pre-existing narrative associations; they can provide a staging ground where
narrative events are enacted; they may embed narrative information within their mise-en-scene; or they provide resources for
emergent narratives." Section headings that follow: EVOCATIVE SPACES, ENACTING STORIES, EMBEDDED NARRATIVES, EMERGENT
NARRATIVES. His closing paragraph names them evoked, enacted, embedded and emergent narratives. (S1, section "SPATIAL STORIES
AND ENVIRONMENTAL STORYTELLING", 4th paragraph; closing paragraph before the notes.)

F2. [read] Evocative spaces build on stories or genre traditions the visitor already knows; such works "draw upon our
previously existing narrative competencies" and can paint in broad outlines and "count on the visitor/player to do the rest."
His example: McGee's Alice rewrites the story "by redesigning Alice's spaces". (S1, EVOCATIVE SPACES, paragraph 1.)

F3. [read] Enacted: in Jenkins's account, spatial stories hang on broad goals and conflicts and move forward as the character
crosses the map; plot becomes designing geography so that obstacles block and affordances help the protagonist's movement.
(S1, ENACTING STORIES, paragraph 2.)

F4. [read] Micronarratives: localized incidents, after Eisenstein's "attraction" ("memorable moments"). Of the Odessa Steps
units, "None of them last more than a few seconds". And: "Micronarratives may be cut scenes, but they don't have to be."
(S1, ENACTING STORIES, paragraph 3.)

F5. [read] Embedded narrative rests on the syuzhet/fabula split: comprehension is an active process of hypotheses from "cues
and clues", and in games "players are forced to act upon those mental maps", testing them against the world. (S1, EMBEDDED
NARRATIVES, paragraph 1.)

F6. [read] Jenkins does not reject exposition outright: "The heavy-handed exposition that opens many games serves a useful
function in orienting spectators to the core premises". (S1, EMBEDDED NARRATIVES, paragraph 1.)

F7. [read] "Read in this light, a story is less a temporal structure than a body of information." A game designer controls
narration "by distributing the information across the game space". (S1, EMBEDDED NARRATIVES, paragraph 2.)

F8. [read] Redundancy: "essential narrative information must be redundantly presented across a range of spaces and artifacts,
since one can not assume the player will necessarily locate or recognize the significance of any given element." He compares
soap operas and "the law of three", by which "any essential plot point needs to be communicated in at least three ways." He
also names designers' "kludges" that steer players toward "narratively salient spaces". (S1, EMBEDDED NARRATIVES, para. 2.)

F9. [read] Two narratives at once: one unstructured and player-controlled, the other pre-structured and embedded in the
scene, waiting to be found. "The game world becomes a kind of information space, a memory palace." "Embedded narrative
can and often does occur within contested spaces", and mixing enacted and embedded elements balances interactivity against a
pre-authored story. (S1, EMBEDDED NARRATIVES, paragraph 3.)

F10. [read] Against Juul's claim that flashbacks are impossible: "Games are no more locked into an eternal present than films
are always linear." ~~Citing Carson, he wants narrative embedded~~ *(second reader: Jenkins gives this as Carson's view: "Carson
suggests that part of the art of game design comes in finding artful ways of embedding narrative information"; the "drug around"
words are Jenkins's own and are not in Carson's 2000 article, whose text has no "neck"; S1 EMBEDDED NARRATIVES para. 4, S5)*
"without giving the player a sensation of being drug around by the neck", and ~~notes~~ *(second reader: "Players, he argues",
that is Carson as Jenkins reports him)* that players can return to a familiar space later and find it transformed by
off-screen events. (S1,
EMBEDDED NARRATIVES, paragraph 4.)

F11. [read] Melodrama: "Melodrama depends on the external projection of internal states". Spaces transformed by narrative
events can carry "loss or nostalgia". In Hitchcock's *Rebecca* the title character never appears, yet every artifact recalls
her: "No matter where she goes in the house, she can not escape Rebecca's memory." (S1, EMBEDDED NARRATIVES, paragraph 5.)

F12. [read] Most embedded narratives "take the form of detective or conspiracy stories", because those genres motivate
examining clues and reconstructing the past. Melodrama is the other, "largely unexplored" model: "as we read letters and
diaries, snoop around in bedroom drawers and closets, in search of secrets". Majestic is his extreme case: documents, codes,
garbled transmissions, a linear story scrambled for the player to rebuild. (S1, EMBEDDED NARRATIVES, paragraph 6.)

F13. [read] On The Sims: houses stripped to a few artifacts, each with one narrative function: "Such choices result in a highly
legible narrative space." He quotes Lynch: "a landscape whose every rock tells a story may make difficult the creation of fresh
stories" (Lynch 1960, p. 6, per his note 28). (S1, EMERGENT NARRATIVES, paragraph 2.)

F14. [read] In his closing, embedded narratives make the game space a memory palace the player deciphers to rebuild the plot,
and designers are best thought of "less as storytellers than as narrative architects." (S1, closing paragraph.)

F15. [read] Two versions exist and differ in small ways. S2 (dated "Saturday, July 10th 2004", marked peer reviewed) reads
"must be presented redundantly" and "because one cannot assume" where S1 reads "must be redundantly presented" and "since one
can not assume". On who coined "ludology", S1 note 1 credits Aarseth; S2 adds an editors' note that Eskelinen says Frasca
introduced it. The four ways sentence is the same in both. (S2, opening paragraph and the redundancy paragraph.)

F16. [summary: search results, unverified against the book] The essay is also printed in Wardrip-Fruin and Harrigan (eds.),
*First Person: New Media as Story, Performance, and Game*, MIT Press 2004, pp. 118-130. (S15.)

F17. [read] The words "instead of forcing it" do not occur in either text. A case-insensitive search for "forc" in S1 and S2
finds only "players are forced to act upon those mental maps" and "reinforce" in a Carson quote. (S1, S2, grep.)

### Carson 2000 (S5): the source Jenkins draws on

F18. [read] Carson's "cause and effect" vignettes: "These are staged areas that lead the game player to come to their own
conclusions about a previous event or to suggest a potential danger just up ahead." Also: "Putting in an element just because
it is "cool" misses a vital opportunity". (S5, section "Storytelling Through Cause and Effect".)

F19. [read] "Following Saknussemm", after Verne: the player is pulled through by bread crumbs a predecessor left. "Whether you
create notes scattered throughout your environments, or have the game player follow the destructive path of some dangerous
creature", the effect heightens the drama. (S5, same section.)

F20. [read] Cause and effect can show time passing: a character returns to a familiar place "only to find it completely
altered", from a cataclysm or "the disappearance of elements remembered from a previous visit". (S5, same section.)

F21. [read] "A large room with a single shaft of light illuminating a solitary prop is more effective than a room filled with
detailed elements." And save "one detailed element for the end of the hallway and let it draw your audience, like a dangling
carrot, into the next space." (S5, lighting and "less is more" passages.)

F22. [read] The first question is "Where am I?": "if your audience can not answer this question in the first 15 seconds, you
are already lost." The next, "What is my relationship to this place?", can be answered by clues in the environment rather than
"lengthy CD liner notes". (S5, opening craft sections.)

### Smith and Worch, GDC 2010 (S3, S4)

F23. [read] Provenance: Harvey Smith writes "Matthias Worch and I gave a talk on Environmental Storytelling" and posts the
slides and notes; the PDF is the compressed version with "slides and speakers' notes together". (S4, post body.) The GDC Vault
copy is behind a login (S15).

F24. [read] Their definition: environmental storytelling is "staging player-space with environmental properties that can be
interpreted as a meaningful whole, furthering the narrative of the game." (S3, PDF p. 16.)

F25. [read] Their four functions: it relies on the player to associate disparate elements as a meaningful whole; it integrates
perception and active problem solving, which builds investment; it "Invites interpretation of situations and meaning according
to players' views and experience"; and it "can help the player navigate an area by telegraphing." (S3, PDF p. 34.)

F26. [read] Why it works: it is active, "The player is *pulling* the narrative." The Law of Closure: in a Fallout 3 scene of
manacles, booze and a teddy bear, "the act happened inside the player's head". They cite McCloud on what happens between the
panels. (S3, PDF pp. 35-36.)

F27. [read] Steve Powers, ~~quoted on the slides~~ *(second reader: quoted in the speakers' notes for PDF p. 37; the slide image
was not seen)*: "Meaningful narrative is inferred by players if you give them cues but leave
them the space to imagine." (S3, PDF p. 37.)

F28. [read] Interpretation diverges from intent: their reading of a BioShock tableau differed from designer Jordan Thomas's.
They accept this because such setups "are not fundamental to understanding the main narrative", and cite Thomas's belief in
"white space". (S3, PDF pp. 28-30.)

F29. [read] When a story affects play it should be unambiguous: blood stains into a bathroom telegraph a monster (Doom 3); a
dead NPC in a fence shows a hazard. (S3, PDF pp. 30-33.)

F30. [read] Craft: establish a discernible chain of events; chart it as a "puzzle structure" in the design docs so level
designers and artists share it; "A single prop can transform the scene." (S3, PDF pp. 42-45.)

F31. [read] The premise rule: "Make sure that all ES moments draw from your story premise, echoing the world at large." "Every
anonymous ES moment wastes the opportunity to say something about the game." A loop: "Premise spawns events", "Events remind
player of premise". (S3, PDF p. 53.)

F32. [read] The danger: "A disconnect between what the player can do and what the game depicts the non-player characters doing
can be jarring." Their cases: lovers dead in an embrace, a sewer ramp for a motorbike the player cannot ride, NPCs crushed by
doors the player uses constantly. (S3, PDF pp. 57-61.)

F33. [read] Systemic storytelling: decals and breakables record the player's history; linear games rarely revisit, so build
revisits. Colantonio, quoted: "It's memorable to come back and see your own mess." Systems can leave items the designer then
reuses, so the player makes the visual language of the authored scenes. (S3, PDF pp. 66-75.)

### Shipped precedents for found text (S13, S14)

F34. [summary: Wikipedia, Outer Wilds, raw wikitext lines 39, 78, citing Beachum's GDC talk and a Noclip documentary] Nomai
writing is "presented as a branching tree of messages" and read with a translator tool. Players "are not forced to read any of
the writing at a specific time or order". Each conversation hints "where previous conversations could be found", and no
conversation is only background lore, so players "would expect that it was all valuable".

F35. [summary: Wikipedia, Gone Home, raw lines 31, 52, citing the New York Times] "Much of the interactivity rests upon looking
at objects and notes within the house." Because not every player finds every clue, the developers made the story "flexible
enough" to be understood without seeing every element.

F36. [summary: Wikipedia, What Remains of Edith Finch, raw line 51, citing PC Gamer] The developers adopted "floating text
captions of the narrative to be seen by the player", which stayed a key element through development.

F37. [summary: Wikipedia, Tunic, raw lines 20, 28] Most of the game's text is in a constructed writing system the player is not
expected to decipher, with only some words shown in the player's own language; play is learned from found manual pages.

F38. [summary: Wikipedia, Hellblade: Senua's Sacrifice, raw line 31] Using "focus" on totems hidden in the world plays a
voiced memory of Druth's stories; activating all forty-four unlocks a bonus cutscene.

F39. [summary: Wikipedia, Heaven's Vault raw lines 25, 27; Chants of Sennaar raw line 38] Decipherment as play: Heaven's Vault
has the player learn a hieroglyphic language with over a thousand words found in the world; Chants of Sennaar has languages
translated from context clues.

F40. [read] Control's narrative designer *(second reader: both quoted phrases are the article writer's words reporting Maggs,
not her quoted speech; S14 body, published 2019-09-17 per the page's own metadata)*: documents and tapes give backstory
"in short doses", but in a game of room-wrecking
combat they were placed "in parts of the game world that weren't likely to be disrupted, but were also easy to find." (S14,
body, Brooke Maggs interview.)

### Legibility and accessibility (S6-S9, S11)

F41. [read] GAG, "Allow players to progress through text prompts at their own pace" (Cognitive, Basic): "it is not possible to
set a speed for text to be displayed which will be appropriate for all readers." "Instead of a timer, dismiss information on a
player action, allowing it to be stepped through." The page states "14% of adults have a reading age of below 11 years" and
gives no source for it on the page.

F42. [read] GAG, "Use an easily readable default font size" (Cognitive and Vision, Basic): from Amazon's 10-foot UI guidance,
28 px at 1080p; "use 28px as a minimum rather than a target, aim to exceed it wherever possible." Small fonts are also harder
for dyslexia. "Allow the font size to be adjusted" is Vision, Advanced.

F43. [read] GAG, "Use simple clear text formatting" (Basic): a clean sans serif with distinct letter shapes; for longer text,
mixed case, left aligned, 1.5 line spacing, "around 70 characters per line". Extreme dyslexia fonts only as an option. The
Last Door: of players who finished, "14% did so using the dyslexia friendly option".

F44. [read] GAG, "Provide high contrast between text/UI and background" (Vision, Basic): "a foreground/background contrast ratio
of at least 4.5:1"; where a plain background is not possible, use outlines and shadows. The page also says high-contrast text
triggering seizures is a misconception.

F45. [read] GAG, "Ensure no essential information (especially instructions) is conveyed by text alone" (Cognitive,
Intermediate): large blocks of text can be "so intimidating that the text is skipped entirely". A player quoted there: "i LOVE
discovering relics of the past but ... i wish all documents could have audio". "Provide pre-recorded voiceovers for all text"
is Advanced.

F46. [read] GAG, subtitles (Hearing, Basic): "Text is presented no smaller than 46px@1080p, either by default or though
options"; on a solid or semi-opaque background; "no more than 40 characters per line, and two lines per subtitle". "Art
direction should not take precedence over readability", and options can satisfy both.

F47. [read] XAG 101: minimum default text, measured as body height (descender plus x-height plus ascender): console 26 px at
1080p; PC/VR 18 px at 1080p. Text should scale to 200 percent of the minimum. "Platform-provided screen magnification tools
aren't an appropriate mitigation for small text size." (S7, 101, "Text size" and "Text scaling"; page metadata updated
2026-06-17.)

F48. [read] XAG 101: "If stylistic fonts are used (for example, blood dripping off font), provide a non-stylized font option."
"Ensure that all fonts include complete character sets for all supported languages." Text in gameplay environments "should
also be accessible by default or configurable"; its example outlines world text. Line width at most 80 characters. (S7, 101.)

F49. [read] XAG 102: standard text at least 4.5:1; large text 3:1, where large on PC is 36 px at 1080p; a high-contrast mode at
7:1. Because "the gameplay environment is in constant visual flux", it suggests letting players "put a solid background behind
any on screen text" or adjust its opacity. (S7, 102, ~~implementation guidelines~~ *(second reader: the flux sentence and the
solid-background option sit under "General approaches to improving contrast"; the 4.5:1, 3:1, 36 px and 7:1 figures sit under
"Implementation guidelines")*.)

F50. [read] WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.3 (AA): text at least 4.5:1, large-scale text 3:1 (large is at least 18 point, or 14 point bold).
Exempt as "Incidental": text that is decoration or "part of a picture that contains significant other visual content". SC
1.4.4: text resizable to 200 percent. (S8.)

F51. [read] WCAG 2.2 SC 2.2.1 Timing Adjustable (A): for any time limit, the user can turn it off, or "adjust the time limit
before encountering it over a wide range that is at least ten times the length of the default setting", or extend it, unless
an exception applies, such as "The time limit is essential and extending it would invalidate the activity". (S8.)

F52. [read] FAA HFDS 2003, §8.2.5.6.5-9 (source given: ANSI 1988): "The character height for maximum legibility and
readability should be 20 to 22 minutes of arc." Minimum where legibility matters: 16 minutes of arc; at least 10 when character
recognition is not time-critical; "The maximum character height for readability shall be 24 minutes of arc." (S11, ch. 8,
printed p. 8-16.) §5.6.1 sets at least 15 minutes for electroluminescent displays (ch. 5, p. 5-17).

### Reading rate and dwell (S9, S10, S12)

F53. [read] BBC: "The recommended subtitle speed is 160-180 words-per-minute (WPM) or 0.33 to 0.375 second per word." Aim for
"a minimum period of around 0.3 seconds per word (e.g. 1.2 seconds for a 4-word subtitle)", with timing an editorial call.
For children under 11, subtitles "should follow the speed of speech", and difficult words should stay on screen as long as
possible. (S9, sections 4, 4.1, 20.2, 20.1.)

F54. [read] Brysbaert: across 190 studies (18,573 participants), adult silent reading in English averages 238 wpm for
non-fiction and 260 wpm for fiction; most adults fall in 175-300 wpm (non-fiction) and 200-320 wpm (fiction). Reading aloud
averages 183 wpm (77 studies). The 238 and 260 figures "are valid for adults between 18 and 60 years without reading problems".
(S10, abstract p. 1; conclusion p. 21.)

F55. [read] Brysbaert: rates "are lower for younger children and older adults" and are "only valid for native speakers". For
second-language readers, "reading rates below 100 wpm are not uncommon": in Hirai (1999), Japanese students reading easy English
prose read at 139 wpm when they answered over 75 % of questions correctly, and at 61 wpm otherwise. Taylor (1965), Table 4:
80 wpm in grade 1, 138 in grade 3, 185 in grade 6, 204 in grade 8, 250 in grade 12, 280 for college. (S10, pp. 16, 19, 21.)

F56. [read] Brysbaert: "Russian needs some 20% fewer words to express a message than English", so Russian wpm runs lower;
languages needing fewer words show lower wpm for the same information. (S10, p. 14; Table 5 and p. 21.)

F57. [read] Majaranta et al.: dwell selects by "a gaze duration that is longer than the normal viewing time". "A long dwell
time is good for preventing false selections but a long fixation on the same target can be tiring to the eyes." Most studies
used a fixed 450-1000 ms. With dwell under the user's control, the mean fell from 876 ms (session 1) to 282 ms (session 10);
all eleven, the outlier included, ended between 180 and 380 ms. Participants "wanted to keep the closing circle" progress
cue *(second reader: the
same passage adds "five participants tried gaze typing without the circle but only two had it turned off in the end of the last
session"; S12 p. 4)*,
and nine rated the click sound as important as or more important than the visual cue. Eye tracker; 11 Finnish students aged
18-30. (S12, pp. 1-4.)

F59. [inferred] In-world text size. With a perspective camera, a screen-centre pixel subtends 2·tan(FOV/2)/W radians. At the

F60. [inferred] The units do not line up. XAG measures body height in screen pixels; the FAA gives "character height" in arc
minutes at the eye, without defining it in §8.2.5.6. On a 24-inch 1080p monitor at 60 cm (pixel pitch 0.277 mm), 18 px is
about 28.5 arcmin and 17 px about 27 arcmin. The two standards can be compared only for an assumed screen and distance.
