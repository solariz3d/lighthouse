# PART ONE

**What the three cases have in common.** In each case, lineages with no shared ancestor that had the trait built the same working solution out of different parts. The octopus eye and the human eye develop from different tissue layers, and the octopus retina is even wired the "right" way round, with no blind spot, while ours is wired backwards. Both still end up with a lens that focuses, an aperture that adjusts, and a light-sensitive sheet at the focal plane. An insect wing is a cuticle membrane with no bones. A pterosaur wing was skin stretched from one enormously long finger. A bird wing is feathers on a fused hand. A bat wing is skin across several spread fingers. All four still make lift with a cambered surface moving through air. Sharks, dolphins and ichthyosaurs drive their tails in different planes and have different skeletons, but they share a torpedo outline: widest about a third of the way back, tapering to a narrow tail stalk, with a crescent-shaped fin. So the materials differ, the history differs and the construction differs, but the solution is the same.

**Why this keeps happening.** Physics sets the problem, and physics allows only a few good answers. Selection is a blind search, but it searches a landscape it didn't make. If you need a sharp image, the optics leave you few choices. You can use a pinhole, which is dim; a lens, which is bright and sharp; a mirror; or many small facets. Once a light-sensitive cup starts to close up and gather more light, a lens is close to the obvious next improvement. Fluid dynamics is just as strict: water is dense, drag grows steeply with speed, and at a given size the low-drag shapes for fast cruising all look alike. So unrelated searches, on a surface with a small number of high points, keep finding the same high points. Different materials just mean the same point was reached from different starting places.

**Where a design like "an eye" or "a wing" belongs.** I don't think it belongs to the animal, and it doesn't really belong to the lineage either. The animal carries one instance of it, and the lineage is the route that happened to get there. The design itself belongs to the problem: a particular medium and a particular physics, together with a particular need, like seeing by light or moving fast through water. It is a location in the space of possible bodies that exists whether or not anything occupies it. Lineages find it rather than own it, much as two mathematicians who have never met can prove the same theorem, and the theorem doesn't belong to either of them. The camera eye was available before any animal had one.

**One further example.** Echolocation evolved separately in bats and in toothed whales. Some cave birds (oilbirds and swiftlets) and shrews have cruder versions. It belongs on this list because the physics of sound sets the task, which is to tell distance and texture from returning echoes in dark or murky conditions. Bats and dolphins even carry some of the same amino-acid changes in prestin, a protein involved in high-frequency hearing. That shows the convergence isn't only in overall shape. In places it reaches down to individual molecules.

**An objection to my own reading.** "Physics picks the answer" is too clean in two ways. First, there is survivorship in what we notice. We list the convergences and pass over the much larger number of problems that got many different solutions. Eyes themselves come in compound, mirror and pinhole forms (the scallop has mirror eyes, the nautilus a pinhole eye). No animal ever evolved a wheel, and no one argues from that that physics forbids wheels. So the honest version is that the landscape has several high points and sometimes a strong one, not a single destination. Second, the lineages are less independent than "unrelated ancestors" suggests. Octopuses and vertebrates both inherited opsin proteins and the same master control gene for eye development (Pax6) from a shared ancestor that had no camera eye. The shared toolkit narrows the search before physics does anything. So part of what looks like the world forcing one answer is really old shared equipment being reused. Separating how much comes from the problem and how much from the inherited toolkit is a real empirical question, and it probably has a different answer for eyes than for fish shapes.
