## PART ONE

**What the first three have in common.** In each of the first three cases, the belief helps make itself true. It shapes behaviour, and the behaviour produces the evidence that then seems to confirm it. The fact isn't simply there waiting to be noticed. The person keeps it going, partly by believing it, and it takes time to build. The beliefs are also about dispositions ("I am the kind of person who…") rather than about what is happening right now, and a disposition can only show up through what you do.

The last three are different. The headache, your height and your present anger exist whether or not you hold the belief. The belief reports on them without producing them. If you stopped believing you were tall, nothing about your height would change. If the public speaker stopped believing they were bad at it, one of the links holding the badness in place would be gone.

**Are they wrong about themselves?** Mostly not about the current facts. The avoidant speaker really does speak badly now. The lonely person really is alone. The error lies in what they take those facts to show. They read an outcome they are maintaining as a nature they discovered. "I am bad at speaking" is heard as "this is fixed in me," when the more accurate version is "this is where my avoidance has left me." So the mistake is about why the thing is true and whether it had to be, not about whether it is true.

The morning person makes the same mistake in a harmless form. They are right, it costs them nothing, and they may just be calling a habit a trait. The "unlovable" case is the worst, because the evidence is contaminated as well. Being left alone after you have withdrawn cannot tell you much about how people would respond if you hadn't withdrawn. The person is testing a hypothesis with data their own belief produced.

**Would a close friend know them better?** On these points, often yes. On the other three, no: nobody knows your headache better than you do. The reason is not that the friend has better access to the person's inner life. It's that the friend's evidence wasn't produced by the belief. The friend may have heard them give a good toast at a wedding when they forgot to be afraid, or watched people warm to them before they pulled back. The friend sees the loop from outside and can see how things might have gone otherwise, which the person inside the loop can't, because the loop filters what they experience. The friend is not infallible. They have their own loops and investments. But their errors are less correlated with the person's, and that is the advantage.

**A further example.** "I'm bad with names." It belongs on the list because the belief gives you permission not to attend at the one moment it matters, when you're being introduced. So you don't take the name in, forget it, and file that as more proof.

**An objection to my own reading.** The line between the two groups is less clean than I drew it. Anger is partly made up of how you interpret your situation. Believing "I'm angry right now" can feed the anger, and deciding "actually I'm just tired" can drain it. Pain is changed by attention and expectation too, which is part of how placebos work. Only height is truly untouched by belief. So the real distinction may be a spectrum: how strongly and how quickly a belief feeds back into the thing it describes. The first three cases are just the slow, strong, behaviour-driven end of that spectrum. If that's right, "are they wrong about themselves?" is the wrong frame for any of the six. For the first three, and in smaller ways for anger and pain, the better question is whether the belief has ever been tested somewhere it wasn't steering the result. Answering that usually means acting against the belief at least once, which is exactly what it discourages.
