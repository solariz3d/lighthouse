# PART ONE

**What the first three share.** In each of the first three cases the belief helps cause the fact it describes. The belief shapes what the person does: they avoid the podium, go to bed early, or withdraw. Those habits pile up over time, and the world responds in kind. The result then comes back looking like independent evidence. The belief works as an input to the fact while presenting itself as a report on it.

The last three are different. Being tall is fixed by bone, whatever you believe. A headache or present anger is an occurrent state. You know it directly, and believing in it doesn't build it up through a history of behaviour. (Anger is the least clean of the three, which I come back to below.)

**Are they wrong?** Not in the same way, and not always.

- **The morning person isn't wrong.** They believe it and it is true. The belief made itself true, and nothing is harmed.
- **The public speaker** is right about the present fact: they do speak badly now. They are wrong about what kind of fact it is. "I am bad at speaking" sounds like a fixed capacity they discovered. In reality it is a skill level they partly produced by avoiding practice. Their error is about the counterfactual: what would be true if they had practised. They are also wrong about their own role, because they file their own causal contribution under "evidence."
- **The unlovable person** makes the same error, and it costs more. Being alone is real. But "unlovable" is a claim about how others would respond if given the chance, and the person is withholding exactly that chance. Their evidence was produced by the test they rigged. It can't tell them anything about the thing they think it shows.

So the error is not about their current condition. It is about the source of that condition and whether it could be otherwise.

**Would a close friend know them better on these points?** Yes. That isn't because the friend has better access to the person's inner life; on the headache the friend knows less. It is because the error sits in a loop that runs through behaviour over time, and behaviour over time is visible from outside. The friend can see the avoidance and remember the times it went fine. They can also notice "you left the party early, then said no one talks to you." They aren't invested in the belief, so they don't file each outcome as confirmation. The person has privileged access to what they feel. They don't have privileged access to the pattern they are making.

**A further example:** the insomniac who believes "I'm a bad sleeper." Believing it makes them anxious at bedtime. They lie there watching the clock, and the anxiety keeps them awake. This belongs on the list because the belief causes the fact directly: the worry about sleeping is part of what stops the sleep. It's a fast version of the same loop, working in one night instead of over years.

**What follows, and one objection to my own reading.**

What follows: for beliefs like these, "is it true?" is the wrong question to ask first, or at least not a sufficient one. The belief is one of the causes of its own truth, so evidence alone can't settle whether to hold it. You also have to ask what holding it will make of you. That doesn't license positive delusion. The loop has limits: believing you're a strong swimmer doesn't keep you afloat. But the space where the loop operates is larger than people tend to think.

The objection: I drew a sharper line than the cases support. I put "angry right now" with the independent facts, but that is too clean. Telling yourself "I'm furious" can feed the anger within minutes, much as the insomniac's belief feeds wakefulness. The first-person reports aren't immune either. Most of them just have a shorter or weaker loop. So the real distinction is probably a matter of degree: how far a belief sits upstream of its own object, and over what timescale. Height is at one end, unlovability at the other, and anger somewhere in the middle, closer to the self-fulfilling end than my first sort allowed.
