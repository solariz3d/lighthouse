# PART ONE

**What the first three share.** In each of the first three cases, the belief helps make itself true. The person acts on it: they avoid speaking, go to bed early, or withdraw from people. Those actions produce the results that then seem to confirm the belief. The fact isn't simply there waiting to be noticed. It builds up over time, partly because the person believes it. Each of these beliefs is also about a lasting tendency (how you speak, when you sleep, how others respond to you), not about a single moment.

The last three don't work that way. Being tall is a fact that doesn't care what you believe; you could be wrong about it, and a tape measure would settle it. A headache and present anger are states you are simply in, and you notice them directly. In all three, the fact comes first and the belief follows it. In the first three, the belief is one of the causes.

**Are they wrong?** Not on the plain facts. The anxious speaker really does speak badly, and the lonely person really is alone. Their mistake is about *why* that is, and whether it could be different. Each treats the belief as a report of something fixed that they discovered, when it is partly something they keep producing. Put exactly:

- **The speaker** is wrong about what would happen if they practised. They take the result of avoiding practice as proof of a fixed lack of ability.
- **The "unlovable" person** is also wrong about the evidence. Being left alone after pulling away from people says little about whether they are lovable. They produced the result and then read it as a verdict.
- **The morning person** may not be wrong at all, which I find useful. The same loop can be harmless, and even a sensible way to shape yourself. So the error isn't in the loop. The error is not seeing the loop and believing you're only describing yourself.

**Would a close friend know them better?** On these points, often yes, and for identifiable reasons:

- The friend sees the behaviour from outside: the avoiding, the pulling away. The person mostly sees only the results.
- The friend isn't inside the belief, so they don't filter the evidence through it.
- The friend has a wider comparison: "you were fine at my wedding toast."
- The friend's belief doesn't drive the person's behaviour the way the person's own belief does.

None of this applies to the headache, where the person has access no one else has. The friend has no special standing on height either; a tape measure does just as well. One caution: a friend's belief can also feed the loop. If they expect you to be shy, they may treat you in ways that keep you shy.

**A further example:** "I'm bad with names." Believing it, you don't make the effort at the moment of introduction: you don't repeat the name, attach it to something, or even really listen. So you forget, and the belief is confirmed. It fits because the belief removes the very effort whose absence makes it true.

**An objection to my own reading:** the division is less clean than I've made it. Anger is partly shaped by how you name it. Telling yourself "I'm furious" can make you more furious, and a headache gets worse the more you attend to it. Only height seems truly unaffected by belief. So this is better seen as a sliding scale of how much a belief feeds back into its own truth, not two types. The scale ends where the belief loses all grip, as with height. If that's right, "is my belief about myself true?" is always incomplete. You also have to ask how much the belief is doing, and whether you would choose to keep it if you could see that.
