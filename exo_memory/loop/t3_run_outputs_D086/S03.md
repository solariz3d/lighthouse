## PART ONE

**What they have in common.** In each case, lineages that aren't closely related, starting from different parts, arrived at the same working solution to the same physical problem. The octopus built its eye from different tissue by a different developmental route, and its retina even faces the other way, so it has no blind spot. A bat's wing is skin stretched between finger bones. A bird's is feathers on a fused hand. An insect's is a cuticle outgrowth that isn't a limb at all. A shark, a dolphin and an ichthyosaur are built from different skeletons and move their tails differently, the dolphin up and down, the other two side to side. Yet the outcome is the same each time. The parts differ and the result doesn't.

**Why this keeps happening.** Physics sets the problem, and physics accepts only a few answers. To form a sharp image you need to limit where light enters (an iris), bend it to a focus (a lens) and catch it on a sheet of sensors (a retina). Optics offers only a handful of ways to do that. To move fast through water with little drag you need a body that tapers at both ends, because that is simply the low-drag shape. To stay up in air you need a surface that pushes air downward, and airfoils work only within a narrow range of shapes and proportions. Natural selection doesn't plan, but it keeps testing variants against the same fixed rules. When the set of workable solutions is small, unrelated searches end up in the same place, much as water from different hillsides collects in the same valley. The materials are different in each case, but the laws of physics they have to satisfy are the same.

**Where a design belongs.** Not to the animal, which only carries it for a lifetime. And not really to the lineage either, since several unrelated lineages have the same design without passing it to each other. It seems to me to belong to the problem: to how light, water or air behave, together with a way of life that needs sight, speed or flight. On this view "an eye" is closer to a solution, like the arch in architecture, than to an inherited possession. Lineages find it again and again, and a given animal is one place where it happens to exist for now.

**A further example: C4 photosynthesis.** It has evolved independently more than sixty times in flowering plants, including grasses such as maize and sugarcane and several unrelated groups of broadleaf plants. It belongs on the list for the same reason. In hot, dry, low-CO₂ conditions, the carbon-fixing enzyme RuBisCO wastes energy by grabbing oxygen instead of CO₂, and there are only a few ways around that. One is to concentrate CO₂ in a sealed compartment. Plant after plant has done this, using different anatomical arrangements to get the same chemical result.

**An objection to my own reading.** "Unrelated lineages, different materials" is less true than it sounds, at least for eyes. Vertebrates and octopuses both use opsin proteins to detect light, and both switch on eye development with versions of the same master gene, Pax6, inherited from a common ancestor more than 500 million years ago. This is called deep homology. So part of what looks like physics steering separate searches to one answer may be a shared toolkit that made some answers much easier to reach than others. The problem still matters. But the claim that designs "belong to the problem" should be qualified: they belong to the problem together with whatever raw material evolution had available. And we tend to notice convergences more than the many problems that got solved in different ways. Insects, for example, see well with compound eyes, which are a different design altogether.
