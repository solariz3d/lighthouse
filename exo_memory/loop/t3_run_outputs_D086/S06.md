# PART ONE

**What the cases have in common.** In each case, lineages that are not closely related, and that started from different body parts, ended up with the same working solution. The octopus eye and the human eye develop from different tissues and are wired differently. The vertebrate retina is mounted backwards, which leaves a blind spot, and the octopus retina is not. Even so, both eyes have a lens that focuses an image, an adjustable aperture, and a light-sensitive sheet at the focal plane. Insect wings are outgrowths of the body wall. A pterosaur wing is skin stretched from one hugely lengthened finger, a bird wing is feathers on a fused hand, and a bat wing is skin stretched between several fingers. All of them are airfoils that make lift. The shark, the dolphin and the ichthyosaur are a fish, a mammal and a reptile, yet all three have the same spindle-shaped body, a pointed front, and a crescent-shaped tail. The histories and the materials differ. What matches is the way each one works.

**Why this keeps happening.** I think the main reason is that the physical world only offers a few good answers to certain problems. There are only a few good ways to form a sharp image from light, and a lens over a receptor sheet is one of the best. Moving fast through water with little drag means solving a fluid-dynamics problem, and that problem has a narrow best answer. Staying up in air means making lift greater than weight, which constrains shape, area and how the wing moves. Natural selection doesn't aim at anything, but it keeps rewarding small improvements, so it acts like a search that climbs toward whatever peaks exist. If there are only a few high peaks, searches that start in different places will tend to reach the same ones. A second, smaller reason is that lineages are not fully independent. Octopuses and vertebrates both use a gene called Pax6 to start building eyes, and they share light-sensitive opsin proteins. So part of the similarity comes from a shared starting toolkit, and the rest comes from shared physics.

**Where a design like "an eye" belongs.** I don't think it belongs mainly to the individual animal or to the lineage. The animal is one instance of the design, and the lineage is one route by which a design was reached. The design itself seems to belong to the problem: to how light, water or air behave, combined with what a living body needs to do in that setting. In that sense "an eye" is closer to a solution that exists in the space of possibilities before anyone reaches it, somewhat as a mathematical result is there to be found. Lineages find designs. They don't own them.

**A further example: C4 photosynthesis.** Plants have evolved C4 photosynthesis independently more than sixty times, in grasses, sedges, amaranths and other groups, and each time the core trick is the same. The plant pumps CO₂ into a set of inner cells so that the carbon-fixing enzyme, Rubisco, is surrounded by CO₂ and not by oxygen. It belongs on this list because the problem is identical every time. Rubisco wastes energy by grabbing oxygen by mistake, especially in hot, dry, low-CO₂ conditions. That chemical fact points to a small number of fixes, and plants keep finding the same one with different anatomical arrangements.

**An objection to my own reading.** The claim that "the design belongs to the problem" may partly come from how we choose to describe things. We notice the convergences because they are striking. We pay less attention to the many problems solved in very different ways. Light has also been handled by compound eyes, the pinhole eye of the nautilus, and mirror eyes in scallops. Moving through the air has been handled by gliding, ballooning spiders, and wind-borne seeds. Whether two eyes count as "the same design" depends on the level of detail we look at. At the level of "a lens that focuses an image" they match. At the level of wiring and development they don't. So a more careful version of my view would be this: physics sets the limits and marks out a few strong peaks, but calling something one design is partly our decision about which features count. The convergences are real, but I may be overstating how few answers the world allows.
