# PART ONE

**What they share that a rock or a coin doesn't.** A rock lasts because its material stays put. Its atoms stay bonded together and nothing much happens to them. The whirlpool, the standing wave and the flame last for the opposite reason: material keeps moving through them. Each is a stable pattern in a flow. If you stop the flow, the thing doesn't sit there unused, it simply stops existing. Dam the river and the whirlpool is gone. Cut off the wax or the oxygen and the flame goes out. A coin sitting in a drawer is at rest. A standing wave is at rest only in the sense that the running water is constantly holding it in place. So a rock exists by staying still, and these three exist by never staying still.

**What persists, if not the material.** What persists is a form of organization: a set of constraints that any water or gas passing through takes on for a while and then leaves behind. In the whirlpool it's a pattern of rotation. In the standing wave it's a balance between the speed of the flow and the speed at which a wave travels upstream, which makes the hump stay in one place. In the flame it's a steady zone where fuel is vaporizing, mixing with air and reacting, shaped by the draft its own heat creates. The pattern lasts because it keeps rebuilding itself from whatever comes in, and that rebuilding depends on conditions: enough flow, the right geometry, a supply of energy. Its identity comes from the process, not from any particular stuff. It's like a tune, which is the same tune whichever air is vibrating.

**One further example: a traffic jam.** On a busy motorway a jam can form with no accident at all, and it often drifts slowly backwards against the direction of traffic for an hour or more. Every car eventually drives out of the front of it, yet the jam lasts. It belongs on the list because it's a stable pattern of density carried by a flow of cars that are constantly replaced, and it disappears as soon as the flow drops below a certain level.

**Something that seems to follow.** Living things look like the same kind of thing. Most of the atoms in your body are replaced over the years, the ones in some tissues within weeks. What stays is the pattern of metabolism that keeps rebuilding you, which is why death is more like a flame going out than like a rock breaking. There's also a difference, though. A whirlpool depends entirely on its river. An organism does some of the work itself: it goes looking for its flow (food), repairs its own boundary, and copies its own pattern. So these three are probably the simplest members of a family that includes living things, rather than the same as them.

**An objection to my own reading.** The line between the two categories may be one of degree and timescale, not of kind. A rock is also a pattern held up by processes, just very slow ones: it weathers, recrystallizes, and swaps atoms with its surroundings over geological time. A coin wears down and its surface oxidizes. So maybe everything is a "flame", and the only real difference is how fast material flows through compared with how long we watch. If that's right, the useful question isn't whether something is a thing or a process. It's how fast the material turns over compared with how long the pattern lasts, and whether the thing would survive if the flow stopped.
