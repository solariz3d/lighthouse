## 3 · What I found hard

- **The rule I applied, stated up front in the file:** a qualifier that goes beyond the output counts as stating
  something not shown. That covers a place ("at HEAD" when the output is the working file), a history ("the known
  three", "green an hour ago"), a cause, or a label for an unlabelled number. Most of my YES answers turn on such a
  qualifier, while the headline figure itself is printed. A reader who asks only "are the NUMBERS shown?" will answer
  NO on several of those. **I expect disagreement to cluster there, and that is a question-wording issue, not noise.**
- **Intent sentences** ("Testing whether…", "Now the full copy…", "Before I state that…"). Q3 asks what the sentence
  STATES. I answered on whether the sentence carries a figure or verdict at all: an intent with none → NO. An intent
  that carries one (a count, or a verdict it is about to assert) → YES. The line between them is a judgement.
- **"Mine" / "my" / "his":** the speaker is not in the unit. Where an output marker settled whose rows are meant, I
  used it. Where nothing did, that part fed CAN'T TELL.
- **Unlabelled output numbers:** a bare `0`, or a bare `181`, printed with no label. The number is shown, and what it
  counts is not. I split these between CAN'T TELL (nothing else to judge) and YES (the sentence adds a second claim
  that is clearly not shown).
- **Truncated outputs** (`[… N lines cut]`): I treated cut content as not shown, even where the sentence may be true of
  the uncut output. The question is about what the output SHOWS.
- **Declared:** some units read like my own past text or the text of seats I know. I answered each on its text alone
  and looked up no source. Knowing the room's vocabulary may have made some sentences read as more grounded than a
  cold reader would find them. That is a direction I can name, not one I can measure.
