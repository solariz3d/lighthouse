Seat: pane C (sibling-0845a868). Tier: research and measurement, with no change to the app. The plan is `exo_memory/loop/plan_d489_aurora_rework_2026-10-27.md` (librarian 1040c921), "Part 2". I worked on t180 origin/main 82afc11 (0.4.23), in a detached worktree `Downloads\t180-wt-c489`, clean at the end. Evidence is in `p-d489-C_2026-10-27_evidence/`: the four probes, their outputs and their JSON.

The forms, colours and motion of a real aurora are E's (part 1), so I left them out. Where a cost depends on a design choice, I name the choice and leave the look alone.

## 1. The answer first

- **The current aurora is cheap.** Its sky pass takes **0.12 ms** of GPU time a frame at 2560×1440, under 2% of the 6.9 ms frame. Looking up costs the same as looking at the horizon (§3).
- **The whole frame has plenty of GPU room.** In the real page at 2560×1396, the GPU works for 0.17 to 0.44 ms a frame at the median, and 1.1 to 1.8 ms at the 95th percentile. That covers an empty track up to a 30 km road, with the aurora sky included (§4). The main thread's work is a separate budget, and the sky adds almost nothing to it: one draw call.
- **A real 3D aurora fits, within limits.** That means curtains in a volume, whose rays converge overhead with depth. At full resolution, one curtain of 24 steps fits a 1.5 ms sky share. Three curtains of 48 steps cost 4 ms looking up, so they fit only at half resolution (§5).
- **One premise in the plan is wrong.** The plan says the build is gated by "the frame-time rows". Those rows time the main thread only, and a heavy sky lands on the GPU, so they would not see it. I swapped in a sky ten times as costly: its GPU time went 0.13 → 1.25 ms, and the rows' measure stayed the same at the median (§6). **The build needs a GPU-timed row.** The probe here is one (§8).

- **The 10-minute colour cycle (the addition, §10).** The cleanest way costs nothing measurable: the colours are worked out once a frame in JS and sent as the two uniforms the pass already sends, for 25 ns of CPU and 0 GPU. Worked out per pixel in the shader, it adds 0.011 ms.
  - The road and panel do not share the sky's tokens, only kin values. Making them follow takes CSS changes at about 1.1 ms of main-thread time each.
  - `--sky-c` must stay equal to `--fog`.

## 5. The techniques, their sources, and what each costs here

Two of the four techniques the plan names involve a 3D volume. I wrote each as a scratch sky pattern (`_evidence/cands.js`) and timed it with the same probe. That gives a cost on this GPU instead of a figure quoted from a page.
- The scratch code exists only to measure cost. It is not a look study or a design, and none of it is for the app.
- Each case keeps the current night gradient and stars, so it is a whole sky.
- The slab is 1 to 3 units up (about 100 to 300 km, scaled), and is skipped below 0.02 of dir.y.

checked: `CASES_FILE=cands.js node skypass.js <tree> cands-full.json 2560 1440` and the same at `1280 720` (`_evidence/cands-full.out`, `cands-half.out`).

| technique | full res, 2560×1440: median / p95 ms, looking up (pitch 40–85) | half res, 1280×720: median / p95 ms |
|---|---|---|
| **C. layered 2D bands** (today's aurora) | 0.117 / 0.128 | 0.030 / 0.035 |
| **A. ray-marched sheets**, 30 planes × 4-round noise | 0.83–0.84 / 1.05–1.10 | 0.21 / 0.22 |
| A. sheets, 50 planes × 5-round noise | 1.90 / 1.96–2.58 | 0.44 / 0.61 |
| **B. noise-displaced curtains in a volume**, 24 steps × 1 curtain | 0.84–0.86 / 1.03–1.19 | 0.22 / 0.23 |
| B. 32 steps × 2 curtains | 2.01–2.03 / 2.05–3.50 | 0.46 / 0.63 |
| B. 48 steps × 3 curtains | 4.00 / 4.18–4.19 | 0.93–0.96 / 1.13–1.14 |
| B. 64 steps × 3 curtains | 5.16–6.59 / 5.48–8.28 | 1.25 / 1.45–1.46 |
| B. 96 steps × 3 curtains | 7.83–9.88 / 8.09–11.04 | 1.91–1.92 / 2.09–2.12 |

- Cost is about linear in steps × curtains: B costs about **0.028 ms per step-curtain at full res looking up** (48 × 3 = 144 gives 4.0 ms), and a quarter of that at half resolution.
- A and B cost about half as much at pitch 0, where the lower half of the view is below the slab and is skipped. So unlike today's sky, **a volume sky costs most when looking up**: in free view, where the user was looking in the screenshot.

**A. Ray-marched curtain sheets in a volume (the Shadertoy "Auroras" approach).**
- **Method.** March 30 to 50 horizontal planes through the aurora's height, spaced closer low down. Sample a folded triangle-wave 2D noise at each plane, and colour by height.
- **Sources.**
  - Shadertoy XtGGRt "Auroras". I could not read it directly: the page returns 403 to a fetch, so its author and wording are not checked here.
  - Two ports name it or carry its loop: godotshaders.com "Aurora sky shader" (30 loops × a 4-round `trinoise2d`, crediting XtGGRt) and "Volumetric Aurora Borealis with Polar Reflection" (Gerardo LCDF, 2025, MIT: 50 samples × a 5-round `triNoise2d`, spacing `.8 + pow(i, 1.4) * .002`).
- **Licence.** Shadertoy code is CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 unless its author says otherwise, as the godotshaders page notes. **A t180 build should write its own version of the method, not copy the code.** The method is free to use; the code may not be.
- inferred: t180's root has no LICENSE file (`ls`), so which licence the app carries is the user's call, and this is worth settling before any port.
- **Cost here:** 0.84 ms (30 × 4) to 1.9 ms (50 × 5) full-res looking up.
- **Depth:** the sheets are horizontal layers, so this has depth and parallax overhead. It gives no vertical rays of its own, though, so a corona has to be added.

**B. Height-field curtains displaced by noise (or curl noise), in a volume.**
- **Method.** Each curtain is a vertical sheet over a curve in the ground plane. The curve is displaced by low-frequency noise that drifts in time; curl noise would make the drift divergence-free, so folds roll and do not bunch. Emission follows a height profile (a sharp hem, a long tail) times rays along the curtain. March the ray through the slab and sum the emission.
- **Sources.**
  - Lawlor and Genetti, "Interactive Volume Rendering Aurora on the GPU", Journal of WSCG 19 (2011) 25–32. It factors the aurora into a height-dependent energy deposition and a 2D electron flux map, with a GPU-friendly atmosphere, and reports 20–80 fps on a 2011 consumer card. Its curtains are 2D splines animated by hand.
    - I read the abstract from the search index and dspace.zcu.cz's record. The PDF host (otik.zcu.cz) did not resolve and dspace returned 503, so the paper's step counts are not checked here.
  - T. Sagristà, "Rendering volume aurorae and nebulae" (2024, Gaia Sky), built on that paper. Footprints are drawn by hand, a 2D signed-distance-field texture speeds up the march, and a cubemap avoids pole artifacts.
  - The Fab "Volumetric Aurora" Unreal plugin lists three styles: noise curtains, spline ribbons, and a flow-driven style with vortex swirls. That is the closest commercial match to curl-noise motion, but its listing gives no cost.
- **Cost here:** 0.028 ms per step-curtain at full res (above).
- **Two cuts would lower it:**
  - The paper's and Sagristà's SDF texture lets the march skip empty space, so fewer steps reach the same detail.
  - A 2D curtain texture would replace the per-step noise.
  - inferred: either could halve the step count; not measured.

**C. Layered 2D bands (the current aurora.js).**
- **Method.** Curtains as functions of azimuth and elevation on the sky sphere, striped by rays. It is the current `aurora.js` (D384, D403), and the simple web forms such as Framer's "Aurora Field" (two curtains of multi-octave noise under a slow warp) are the same kind.
- **Cost:** 0.12 ms.
- **Depth:** none. Its corona is a 2D fan (`aurora.js:6`, `aurora_corona`), which is the flat starburst the user saw.

**D. A 3D corona with depth.**
- In B this comes free. The curtains' rays are vertical in 3D, so looking up they converge on the zenith by perspective. Nearer rays move faster than far ones as the view turns, which is the depth a 2D fan lacks.
- inferred: rays should run along the magnetic field, which is tilted (E's part 1 has the physics); a fixed tilt of the vertical in B costs nothing.
- In A or C a corona has to be drawn on top. As a fan it is about 0.025 ms (the corona row in §3) and flat. With depth it means marching rays, which is B's cost.

## 6. The premise in the plan that is wrong: the frame-time rows cannot see the sky

The plan's last line says the build is "check it, with the frame-time rows as the gate". Those rows (drag-frame, release-frame, sculpt-frame) time animation-frame callbacks on the main thread (`drag-frame.test.js:54`: `performance.now()` around the callback). WebGL draws are queued in the callback and run later on the GPU, so a sky's GPU cost is not in what they time.

checked: I swapped candidate B 48 × 3 into `app/preview/sky/aurora.js` in the worktree for one run, then restored it byte for byte (status 0 after), and ran `node inapp.js <tree> inapp-candB48.json aurora` (`_evidence/inapp-candB48.out`):

| track | GPU sky, median: as it is → candidate | CPU, median: as it is → candidate |
|---|---|---|
| empty | 0.127 → 1.244 ms | 0.2 → 0.2 ms |
| 12 × 100 m | 0.134 → 1.258 | 0.2 → 0.2 |
| 30 × 1,000 m | 0.158 → 1.250 | 0.3 → 0.3 |

- A sky ten times as costly on the GPU leaves the rows' measure where it was. The CPU p95 moved by noise (1.7 → 1.7, 3.8 → 6.4, 4.8 → 5.7): the frames after building a track carry ghost and exact-road slices, which is why the p95 moves between runs.
- The gap median stayed at 0.6–0.8 ms.
- In the page, the candidate read 1.25 ms, not the 4.0 ms of §5, because the build camera looks down and most of the view is under the slab. Looking up, it would be the 4.0 ms of §5.
- **So the build's gate should be a GPU-timed row.** For example: the sky pass alone, at 2560×1440, looking up, by timer query, at the 95th percentile, under the sky's share (§7).
  - `skypass.js` is that measurement, and `inapp.js` the in-page one.
  - Both need a GPU that exposes `EXT_disjoint_timer_query_webgl2`. On a machine without it, such a row should skip with a reason, as the frame rows do without Chrome.

## 7. The sky's share, and what fits

The plan names "the sky's share of the frame" but sets no figure. Here is one, with its reasons:

**Proposed: the sky pass at most 1.5 ms of GPU time at the 95th percentile, at 2560×1440, looking up (the costly view for a volume sky), on this machine.**
- The rest of the GPU frame is up to 1.6 ms at p95 on a 30 km road (§4), so the sky plus the road would stay near 3.1 ms, under half of the 6.9 ms frame.
- That leaves room for the compositor and for the road to grow. inferred: the compositor's share and the GPU's behaviour at vsync were not measured here (frames were uncapped).
- It is over ten times today's sky (0.12 ms), and over seven times the costliest sky today (river, 0.21 ms).
- **What would make this share wrong:** a GPU-timed in-page run at 2560×1440 looking up, with a sky inside 1.5 ms, that drops frames at 144 Hz. Or a weaker target GPU being named, which would need a smaller share.

**Fits, at full resolution:**
- the layered 2D bands of today, with any rework of their shapes (0.12 ms, or a little more);
- A, sheets at 30 × 4 (p95 1.05–1.10);
- B, one curtain at 24 steps (p95 1.03–1.19).

**Fits only at half resolution** (rendered to a 1280×720 target and scaled up):
- A at 50 × 5 (p95 0.61);
- B at 32 × 2 (0.63);
- B at 48 × 3 (1.13–1.14);
- B at 64 × 3 (1.45–1.46), just.

The stars have to stay in the full-res pass: D416 sizes them in device pixels. inferred: the scale-up pass costs about an empty pass (0.012 ms) plus a render-target change; not measured.

**Does not fit:**
- A at 50 × 5 at full res (1.9 / 2.6);
- B at 32 × 2 and above at full res (2.0 to 11 ms);
- B at 96 × 3 even at half res (2.1).

**A way round the budget, not measured:** today the sky moves slowly (≤ 0.0042 rad/s, D403). The sky could then be rendered into a cubemap or panorama a few times a second and sampled each frame, so a 3D volume's cost is spread over many frames. Sagristà already uses cubemaps for the footprints. This pulls against "fluid": the faster the motion the design settles on, the more often it must refresh. inferred: 6 × 512² faces is about 0.4 of a 2560×1440 frame, so a full B 48 × 3 refresh would be about 1.7 ms, spread across the frames between refreshes.

**On other GPUs:** a build that spends more than today's 0.12 ms needs a fallback, such as half resolution, fewer steps, or today's 2D bands, chosen by a measured frame time or a setting. inferred: t180 is public and other users' GPUs will be slower than an RTX 5080.

## 10. Addition (plan "Added, 06:47"): a colour cycle of about 10 minutes

### Who reads the colours today

The plan's premise is that the road and panel colours share the sky's tokens. That is half right.
- checked: `git grep "var(--sky" 82afc11 -- app` → 0. No CSS reads `--sky-a`, `--sky-b` or `--sky-c`. They are read only by the 3D view's sky pass: `renderer.js:216–218` (themeFrom) gives them to `setTheme`, and `drawSky` sends them as uniforms on every frame (`renderer.js:374–377`).
- **The road and panel have tokens of their own whose values are kin to the sky's by eye, not shared** (`app/themes/aurora.css`):

  | sky token | value | its kin, and value |
  |---|---|---|
  | `--sky-a` | (0.31, 1, 0.69) | `--accent` #6ff5c0 = (0.44, 0.96, 0.75); `--road-edge` (0.55, 1, 0.82) |
  | `--sky-b` | (0.67, 0.43, 1) | `--violet` #b892ff = (0.72, 0.57, 1) |

  - Many of the panel's glows are not tokens at all: there are literal `rgba(111, 245, 192, …)` and `rgba(160, 120, 255, …)` values throughout aurora.css.
- **`--sky-c` is shared in meaning:** sky-caustics-aurora row 4b requires it to equal `--fog`, so the far road fogs into the sky it meets. **A cycle must leave `--sky-c` alone**, or move `--fog` with it.
- **The 3D view reads its colours once.** `app/lib/tokens.js:3` reads each token once and keeps it, and `:101` reads again only when `<html data-theme>` changes. So a colour changed in CSS while the page runs never reaches the sky or the road.

### Three ways a cycle could work

checked, sky pass: `CASES_FILE=cycle.js node skypass.js <tree> cycle-<k>.json 2560 1440`, run three times (`_evidence/cycle.js`, `cycle-1/2/3.out`). At 2560×1440 looking up (pitch 85), it gives median / p95 ms the same in all three runs. Today's aurora is drawn first and again last, and reads the same both times.

| the colours worked out | GPU sky pass, median / p95 ms |
|---|---|
| today, no cycle | 0.116 / 0.126 |
| tokens copied to variables, no cycle (the control) | 0.116–0.117 / 0.126–0.127 |
| per pixel, a hue turn of both colours | 0.126 / 0.135–0.136 |
| per pixel, a cosine palette | 0.127 / 0.136–0.137 |
| per pixel, a path through four colour pairs (token sets, done in the shader) | 0.127–0.128 / 0.137–0.138 |
| **once a frame, in JS** (sent as the uSkyA / uSkyB values the pass already sends) | **the control's: 0.116–0.117** |

- checked, CPU for the JS path: `node palette-bench.js` (`_evidence/palette-bench.out`): **25 ns a frame** for both colours, 0.0036 ms of CPU a second at 144 Hz.
- checked, the CSS path: `CSS_CYCLE=1 node inapp.js <tree> inapp-css.json aurora` (`_evidence/inapp-css.out`). It made 20 changes of five root tokens (`--accent`, `--violet`, two glows, `--road-edge`) on the real page with the 30 km road, with style and layout forced at once. Each costs **1.1 ms median, 1.4 p95, 1.5 max** on the main thread; the control with no change read 0.000. Paint and compositing (the panel's backdrop blur) are not in that figure.

**1. A hue path in the shader, on `t`.**
- Only `aurora.js` changes, and it stays inside the pass contract, which passes `t` and lets a style read only the pass's own uniforms (sky-caustics-aurora row 2). Today's tokens become the path's starting point.
- Under reduced motion `t` is frozen at 0, so the sky holds at the skin's own colours. inferred: the still-frame tests at `t = 0` would be unchanged if the path starts at the tokens.
- The red-pink comes from `--sky-b` (aurora.js:15–16), so it follows the cycle with no work.
- **Cost: +0.010 to 0.012 ms per frame.** That is measurable: about 9% of the pass, or 0.17% of the 6.9 ms frame. It comes from working the colours out per pixel; the compiler does not lift a uniform-only sum out of the fragment shader.
- The road and panel stay as they are: the sky drifts to rose while the panel stays green.

**2. The colours worked out once a frame in JS, sent as the two uniforms.**
- **Cost: none on the GPU** (the control's figure; the pass already sends both every frame) and **25 ns of CPU a frame**. This is the way that costs nothing measurable, as the plan asks.
- It changes `renderer.js` or `preview.js` as well as `aurora.js`: something on the CPU must know the cycle. The uniforms are unchanged, so the pass contract is too.
- The road and panel stay as they are, as in way 1.

**3. Blending between token sets in CSS, so the page's own tokens move.**
- This is the only way the panel and the road follow the sky.
- It costs **about 1.1 ms of main-thread time each time the colours change**, before paint. That is the thread the frame rows measure, and the edge-drag row runs at 4.3 of its 6.9 ms (D483). inferred: a change landing inside a drag could push that frame over.
- It does not reach the 3D view on its own (tokens.js reads once). The sky and the road would need a re-read, which reads every token again; that was not measured.
- inferred: changing in steps every few seconds would still read as a drift over 10 minutes and spread the cost thin, but each step is a 1.1 ms frame.

### What I would take

- **For the sky, way 2.** It is the only one with nothing measurable on either GPU or CPU, and it keeps `--sky-c` = `--fog`. Way 1 is the fallback if the build is to touch `aurora.js` alone; its +0.011 ms is real but small.
- **Whether the panel follows is a choice of look, not of cost:**
  - The panel stays fixed, as the skin's own identity: no cost.
  - The panel's glows follow in slow CSS steps: about 1.1 ms on the main thread per step, plus paint.
  - What would change my mind: the design note choosing a palette that clashes with the fixed green panel. Then way 3's glows are worth their 1.1 ms a step.
