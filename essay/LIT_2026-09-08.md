## 0 · THE FALSIFIER, SCORED FIRST

> **Registered (PROGRAM_BRIEF, P-LIT):** *if MacKay or Ismael already state self-knowledge sorted
> by a graded feedback quantity, the essay's originality claim is gone and the paper becomes a
> reconstruction — which is fine, but must be said.*

**It fires on MacKay. It does not fire on Ismael.** And a third source the falsifier was not
pointed at — Hacking — carries a graded feedback quantity too, with a gradient that runs *against*
the essay's leading prediction.

**MacKay 1960, p. 37**, in the section titled *Criteria of logical indeterminacy*, does all of the
following on one page:

> "Consider now the class of statements that describe the subject's own brain-processes or
> activities causally linked therewith. Among these, there will be a subclass which cannot be
> formulated or cannot be believed by the subject, without brain-activity that interferes with the
> brain-process they describe. **It is unnecessary to assume that all such interference nullifies
> the statement.** … One of the best ways of making an angry man more angry may be to tell him he is
> getting angry! What we can always say is that in such a case the formulating or the believing of
> the statement becomes **one of the factors determining its truth or falsehood**, so that it cannot
> claim unique factual status in isolation."

That is (a) the sorting principle — self-descriptions are sorted by whether representing feeds back
into the represented; (b) an explicit statement that the feedback **comes in degrees**, since not
all of it nullifies; (c) the essay's own sentence — *"it is the worse instrument exactly where it is
one of the makers"* — in MacKay's words, *"one of the factors determining its truth or falsehood."*
He then draws the threshold cases: where formulating is "by itself sufficient to make it false … an
up-to-date formulation becomes impossible to the subject," and where believing is sufficient to make
it false, "the statement for the subject is **logically incredible**" (both p. 37). The zero-gain
contrast is on p. 32: *"whereas my hair or my teeth are substantially unaffected if someone tells me
what he believes about them, my brain, as the organ of understanding, is in a fundamentally
different situation."* Hair and teeth are the read regime, named as such, in 1960.

**What MacKay does not do**, and this is the whole of what survives as new: he never makes the
quantity a **scalar**; he never sorts a **range of standing variables** by its magnitude (his domain
is occurrent brain-processes and imminent choices, never traits); he has **no constitutive cell**;
and he generates **no empirical prediction about self-report versus informant accuracy**.

**And the part that costs the essay most is not the anticipation, it is where MacKay stopped.** He
derives from the loop an *indeterminacy*, and he explicitly refuses the inference to the agent being
*wrong*: "the truth would seem to be that at this point there is no gap in his knowledge; the place
of the onlookers' knowledge is already preoccupied for him by the knowledge that the choice awaits
his decision" (pp. 36–37). The onlookers hold something the agent cannot hold, and the agent is
nevertheless **not ignorant**; the two descriptions stand in a transformation rule under which "it
may be essential for A's belief to differ from B's in order that both may be valid" (p. 39).
**That is REFEREE_A3's finding 1 — the loop gives under-determination, not inaccuracy — reached in
1960 by the nearest predecessor, and taken by him as the correct stopping point rather than a gap to
be filled.** Any §4 that goes past it has to say why MacKay was wrong to stop, not merely add a
premise that lets it walk on.

**Verdict.** The paper is a reconstruction **in its sorting principle** and original **in its
scaling, its third cell, and its prediction**. The honest form of the originality claim is not "a
single quantity that sorts all of these cases" — that quantity is MacKay's — but: *MacKay's
interference condition, made scalar, extended from occurrent processes to standing dispositions, and
turned into a comparative accuracy prediction against an informant.* Stated that way it is smaller,
true, and citable. Stated the current way a judge who knows MacKay stops reading at §4.

## 1 · MacKay 1960

**D. M. MacKay, "III.—On the logical indeterminacy of a free choice," *Mind* LXIX(273), Jan. 1960,
pp. 31–40. Read in full, all ten pages.**

MacKay's target is the argument that if brain processes are determinate, a fully informed observer
could know my choices in advance and my sense of freedom is an illusion of ignorance (p. 31). His
first move is that a prediction of my brain activity must allow for the effect of its own
communication on that brain, "but these effects could not all in general be calculated unless the
prediction itself were already known, so that in general the exact calculation can never be
completed" — the reason is "not primarily psychological but logical" (p. 32). Grant the observers
secrecy and the prediction becomes valid **for them and not for me**: "the onlookers' view
represents a true description of the state of affairs only for the onlookers, since if it were
universally true, A would be wrong not to believe it" (p. 35). He then denies that the agent is
thereby *ignorant*: the proposition "would lose its factual status if A were to entertain it. In
short, the onlookers have no predictive information to give A, even if they would" (p. 36). §7
generalises this into the criterion quoted in §0 above (p. 37), §8 positions it against Ryle's
elusiveness — MacKay agrees with *Concept of Mind* p. 197 that self-elusiveness does not make one
unpredictable to others, and disagrees with Ryle's "having to overlook at least one of the data"
gloss (p. 38) — and the conclusion names the result "a kind of philosophical Principle of
Relativity" in which "the validity and meaningfulness of a belief may depend in a definite and
rigorous way upon who entertains it" (p. 39).

**Add or fail:** the gain sort **fails to add the sort itself** — MacKay p. 37 already sorts
self-descriptions by whether, and how much, formulating or believing one is "one of the factors
determining its truth," and it inherits, unanswered, his reason for stopping at indeterminacy rather
than error (pp. 36–37).

## 2 · Ismael 2007

**J. T. Ismael, *The Situated Self*, OUP 2007 — BOOK NOT REACHED. Substituted, and named as such:
(a) the publisher's preview, containing front matter and the whole of ch. 1 *Introduction*, pp.
i–xii and 3–9, plus the opening of ch. 2, pp. 11–14; (b) Ismael's own *Précis of The Situated Self*,
*Philosophy and Phenomenological Research* LXXXII(3), May 2011, pp. 733–758, read in full.**

The book's stated project is not epistemic. Its subject is the set of pressures that make selves
look unassimilable to the natural order — "the intimacy of reflexive representation and its immunity
to error, the privacy and the irreducible quality of our mental lives, the ability to separate
oneself in imagination from all of one's properties, and the simplicity and apparent unanalyzability
of the notion of a self" — and its claim is that these are "formal difficulties rooted in the
structure of reflexive thought" that dissolve on an embedded, embodied view (p. 3). The feedback
loop is there and is central: a representation "connected in the wider domain, and that is turned
back on it representing that activity, creates a feedback loop," whose "power … to produce new forms
of flexibility in behaviour … gives its dynamical raison d'être" (précis, p. 743). But the work the
loop does is **dynamical and semantic, not epistemic**: the self-modelling "superloop" consolidates
information and centralises control (précis, p. 738), and her *fixed points* are fixed points of the
**representation relation** — points where "the dichotomy between what is doing the representing and
what is being represented" breaks down, illustrated by −40° being the fixed point of the
Celsius/Fahrenheit relation, licensing "semantic descent" between levels (précis, p. 744). That is a
different object from a fixed point of a reaction function from representation to fact.

**Measured, not asserted:** across all 26 pages of Ismael's own précis of the whole book, the strings
*self-knowledge*, *authority*, *asymmetr\** (except once, of the representation relation itself, p.
744) and *immun\** do not occur. On the evidence I opened, she states no epistemic sort at all,
graded or otherwise. **Limit: the précis is a summary, and I did not open ch. 5 (§5.4 "Internal
World-Models and Self-Representational Loops," p. 53), ch. 7 ("Self-Representation, Objectivity, and
Intentionality," p. 75, incl. §7.3 "Why Do We Self-Represent?", p. 83) or ch. 11, which are where
such a sort would live if it is anywhere.** An absence claim from a précis is weak evidence and I am
not upgrading it.

**Add or fail:** against Ismael the gain sort **adds the epistemic dimension entire** — she supplies
the self-modelling loop and a fixed-point vocabulary, and no ordering of self-knowledge by anything
— but the essay must stop borrowing her word, because her "fixed point" is a semantic level-crosser
and not the essay's equilibrium.

## 3 · McGeer 1996

**Victoria McGeer, "Is 'self-knowledge' an empirical problem? Renegotiating the space of
philosophical explanation," *The Journal of Philosophy* XCIII(10), Oct. 1996, pp. 483–515. Scanned
without a text layer; I rendered pages to images and read pp. 483, 512 and 515. THE OTHER 30 PAGES
ARE UNREAD.**

McGeer opens on the puzzle the essay opens on: self-knowledge "seems peculiarly direct and certain,"
we are "assumed to be in a privileged (epistemic) position with respect to the contents of our own
minds" (p. 483), and yet "if coming to know things is an achievement, we seem to achieve best what
we work at least" (p. 483). Her resolution is that the first-person claim is not a report at all.
Folk psychology, on her account, is "successful primarily because we become the kind of agent it
successfully predicts and explains by molding ourselves and others to suit its generalizations";
"a primary use of folk psychology is normative"; and therefore "**Minds are as much made as
discovered**" (all p. 512). The conclusion states the mechanism and the asymmetry together: "our
first-person psychological claims underwrite judgments that we know our own minds, even though our
first-person claims are not themselves expressions of true, justified beliefs about our own
first-order intentional states. We know our own minds because we have been trained to take on the
responsibility … for suiting our words to our deeds and our deeds to our words. Once we admit this —
that 'knowledge' of our own minds is peculiarly dependent on our role as agents — we can readily
explain, **as Ryle could not**, the *principled* difference in authority attaching to first- and
third-person psychological claims" (p. 515).

**Add or fail:** the gain sort **fails to add the made regime** — McGeer already derives the
first/third-person asymmetry from the self-ascription helping make the fact, and does it against the
same Ryle the essay uses — and **adds** only the graded case McGeer has no room for, since on her
account the loop is what *underwrites* authority whereas the essay needs a region where the same
loop *destroys* it.

## 4 · Coliva 2016

**Annalisa Coliva, *The Varieties of Self-Knowledge*, Palgrave Macmillan 2016. BODY NOT REACHED —
the book is paywalled and no legitimate full text was available from this machine. Substituted, and
named as such: the publisher's free front-matter PDF, pp. i–xvi, which carries the series preface
(p. v), the credits (p. vii), the acknowledgments (pp. ix–x) and the complete table of contents with
every chapter's and section's printed start page (pp. xiii–xvi). Every Coliva claim below is read
off that table of contents, i.e. off her own section titles and their page numbers, and nothing
below is read off her argument.**

The architecture is a taxonomy by **kind of mental state**, not a magnitude. Ch. 2 *Varieties of
Mental States* (p. 19) partitions the domain first — perceptions and sensations (p. 20),
propositional attitudes split into **dispositions** (p. 27) and **commitments** (p. 31), emotions
(p. 38). Ch. 3 *Varieties of Self-knowledge* (p. 51) then partitions the epistemology to match:
first-personal self-knowledge under groundlessness (p. 52), transparency (p. 58) and authority
(p. 62) — and, separately, **"3 Third-personal Self-knowledge" (p. 69)**. Chs. 4–7 are the rivals
(inner sense, inferential, simulation, p. 77 ff.; Peacocke, Burge, Fernández, Moran, p. 99 ff.;
expressivism, Wittgenstein and Bar-On, p. 133 ff.; constitutivism — Shoemaker, Wright, Bilgrami and
Coliva's own, p. 163 ff.). The thesis is ch. 8, *Pluralism About Self-Knowledge* (p. 217), whose
three sections are the three cells: "§1 Propositional Attitudes as Commitments: The Limits of the
Constitutive Account" (p. 219); "§2 Sensations, Basic Emotions, Perceptions and Experiences:
Constitutivism Meets Expressivism" (p. 222); and — the one that matters here — **"§3 Propositional
Attitudes as Dispositions and Complex Emotions: Third-personal Self-knowledge" (p. 232)**.

That last title is the finding. Coliva's taxonomy already routes **dispositions** — which is what
"is a person who calls people back", "is a morning person", "is intellectually able" all are — to
**third-personal** self-knowledge, i.e. to the cell where you know yourself the way others know you.
That is the essay's looped-regime informant advantage, reached by attitude type instead of by gain,
and published in 2016. So the essay's first divergent prediction does not separate it from Coliva
either, unless it can name a case the two sorts assign differently and test that.

**Add or fail:** the gain sort **adds a single ordering axis** where Coliva has a partition by
state-type, and so could in principle predict *within* her cells; but it **fails to argue that
one scalar beats her pluralism**, which is the argument REFEREE_A3 asked for, and it cannot claim
the informant-advantage cell as its own, because ch. 8 §3 has it.

## 5 · Hacking 1995

**Ian Hacking, "The looping effects of human kinds," ch. 12 of Sperber, Premack & Premack (eds),
*Causal Cognition: A Multidisciplinary Debate*, Clarendon/OUP, pp. 351–383. Read in full,
all 33 pages.**

The chapter is announced as being "about feedback effects in cognition and culture" (p. 351). Its
domain is *human kinds* — "kinds of people and their behaviour which (it is hoped) can enter into
practical laws" (p. 360) — and the mechanism is stated at p. 370: classification changes the
individuals classified, "which means that the kind itself becomes different"; the changed kind
yields new knowledge, which "in turn becomes part of what is to be known about members of the kind,
who change again. **This is what I call the looping effect for human kinds.**" Restated on the same
page: "New sorting and theorizing induces changes in self-conception and in behaviour of the people
classified. Those changes demand revisions of the classification and theories … loop upon loop."
Two further structural claims are directly load-bearing for the essay. First, **Hacking grades the
loop, and names the gradient**: "**The greater the moral connotations of a human kind, the greater
the potential for the looping effect**" (p. 370), with the value-neutral census kinds offered as the
weak-effect comparison on the same page. Second, he cuts the domain by **uptake**: kinds whose
members "cannot take in how they are classified" he calls *inaccessible kinds*, and of them
"**There cannot be self-conscious feedback**" (p. 374) — though looping can still run through a
larger unit such as the family. The epistemic consequence he draws is the one the essay wants:
"the known may overpower the knowers" (p. 360).

**Add or fail:** the gain sort **adds the intra-personal case** — Hacking's loop runs from a
*classification held by others* to the person, not from a person's self-representation to their own
variables — but it **fails to notice that its nearest predecessor already grades the loop, and
grades it by moral connotation**, which is Vazire's *evaluativeness* under another name; on
Hacking's gradient the essay's flagship prediction (informant advantage on **non-evaluative** looped
variables) is a prediction about the corner where the loop should be **weakest**, and the essay
must either take that on or drop the word "non-evaluative".

## 6 · Swann 1983

**W. B. Swann, Jr., "Self-verification: bringing social reality into harmony with the self," in Suls
& Greenwald (eds), *Social Psychological Perspectives on the Self*, vol. 2, Erlbaum, pp. 33–66.
Scanned without a text layer; I rendered pages to images and read pp. 33, 34, 40 and 44. THE OTHER
30 PAGES ARE UNREAD.**

The chapter's purpose is "to examine the processes whereby people work to ensure the stability of
their self-conceptions," processes that "enable people to create — both in their actual social
environments and in their own minds — a social reality that verifies and confirms their
self-conceptions" (p. 33). The motive is explicitly not flattery: "People seem to possess an inborn
preference for things that are predictable, familiar, stable, and uncertainty reducing" (p. 34).
The evidence that matters here is p. 40. In Swann & Hill (1982), participants pre-measured on
self-perceived dominance played a game with a confederate who told some of them "You really seem to
be the forceful, dominant type" and others "You really don't seem to be the forceful, dominant
type." Confirming feedback was passively accepted; disconfirming feedback produced vehement
resistance, and — Table 2.2, means as rated by objective observers — "self-conceived dominants who
were labeled submissive became all the more dominant. At the same time, **self-conceived submissives
who were labeled dominant became especially submissive**" (dominants 36.00 consistent / 46.29
discrepant; submissives 37.20 / 30.00). The maintenance machinery is catalogued at p. 44:
participants "endorse the validity of tests only if the scores they receive fit with their
self-conceptions" (Crary 1966; Korman 1968; Markus 1977), discrepant feedback is dismissed by "a
wide array of mental gymnastics," and self-discrepant feedback is recalled worse than congruent
feedback (Suinn, Osborne & Page 1962).

**Add or fail:** here the gain sort **fails outright and a sentence has to be withdrawn** — the
essay says *"A motivational account has no room for a stable, evidence-proof belief that you are
worse than you are,"* and Swann p. 40 is a motivational account in which a non-flattering self-view
both resists disconfirming evidence and *causally drives the behaviour that confirms it*, i.e. a
motivational mechanism producing exactly the two-sided, evidence-proof lock-in the essay claims as
its structural signature; the second divergent prediction therefore separates the essay from
Vazire's self-enhancement mechanism only, not from motivational accounts, which is REFEREE_A3
finding 3's version and it is correct.

## 7 · TWO CORRECTIONS TO THE REFEREE'S OWN CHARACTERISATIONS

REFEREE_A3 ran offline and flagged that its citations were checked against memory. Two of them do
not survive contact with the pages, and both corrections make the referee's *own* case stronger
rather than weaker, which is why they are worth carrying.

1. **"MacKay … argued with exactly the fixed-point structure" (finding 3) is not what the 1960 paper
   does.** The strings *fixed point*, *equilibrium*, *degree*, *magnitude* and *graded* do not occur
   anywhere in pp. 31–40. MacKay's argument is self-referential and logical, not dynamical: the
   defect is in the *validity of the act of believing* a proposition, not in the failure of a map to
   have a fixed point. The correct claim is stronger and stranger — MacKay gets the essay's sorting
   principle **without** the fixed-point machinery, which means the 1954 Grunberg–Modigliani/Simon
   import is doing less work in the essay than it appears to.

   limit" is unevidenced on what I could open.** The self-modelling dynamical system is certainly

   is MacKay's name. It may be in a chapter I did not open; on the record available it should be
