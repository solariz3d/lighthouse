I.

There is a consolation folded into the premise, and it is the first thing to get rid of. If a self is the parameters of a process rather than the state it happens to be holding, then nothing that merely empties the state can destroy the self. Memories can go, projects can be interrupted, the whole current contents of a life can be scattered, and the thing that was converging is still there, still converging. What you are cannot be stolen because it was never in the box.

This inference is valid. It is also an answer to a question no one was asking. Nobody grieves a state as such. Nobody mourns the particular contents of last Tuesday. What we fear when we fear loss is not the deletion of records but something about the record-keeper, and the premise, far from making that fear unfounded, gives it a much sharper form than the ordinary view can. My thesis is that the parameter view does not shrink loss. It relocates it — and the losses it relocates to are worse than the ones it dissolves, in three specific ways. They are invisible from the inside. They are misremembered from the outside. And they can occur without any damage to the thing lost.

I want to end with a positive characterization: on this view, loss is not subtraction but narrowing. The measure of a loss is the size of the set of situations the process can no longer meet.

II.

Start with the relocation itself, because the premise gets some real empirical credit here before it starts costing us anything.

Severe amnesia is the case where the state view predicts annihilation and gets it wrong. Clive Wearing, the musician whose encephalitis left him with a memory measured in seconds, kept almost nothing of what the state view calls a person: no continuous narrative, no retrievable past, a diary of repeated first awakenings. But he kept playing and conducting, and he kept greeting his wife with undiminished joy each time she entered, as though after years apart. The dispositions held. The state was gone and the shape was not. Families of amnesiacs standardly report that the person is *there* — the humor lands the same way, the tastes are the same tastes, the characteristic irritations are characteristic. The archive burned; the archivist survived.

The mirror case is the one that should unsettle us. After certain frontal injuries, patients keep everything the state view counts: continuous memory, intact knowledge, fluent report of who they are and what happened to them. And families say, with a consistency that ought to be treated as evidence, *that is not him*. They are told this is a figure of speech. On the premise assumed here it is not a figure of speech. It is a literal and correct identification of what was destroyed, made by the people best placed to detect it, in the only vocabulary available to them.

So the relocation is not a philosopher's redescription. It tracks a distinction that people already make and are usually talked out of. What we fear in dementia, when we are honest, is not the forgetting. It is the arrival of a stranger who remembers.

III.

Now the cost. If the self is the parameters, and the parameters are what does the noticing, then parameter loss is systematically undetectable by the thing that suffers it.

Detecting a loss requires a comparison, and a comparison requires a comparator that stays fixed across the two terms. When the state changes, the comparator is intact: I reach for the name and find a gap, and the gap is legible as a gap because the reaching is unchanged. When the parameters change, the comparator has changed with them. There is nothing left inside that stands outside the change to register it. The result is not that parameter loss goes unnoticed. It is worse than that: parameter loss gets reliably *reported as something else*. It presents itself as a fact about the world.

The depressed person does not experience a contraction of their capacity for interest. They experience a world that has become uninteresting, and they will defend the observation with arguments, because from inside the new parameters the arguments are sound. The person whose generosity has been worn away by a decade of small betrayals does not notice a diminished disposition to give. They notice, correctly by their lights, that people have turned out not to deserve it. The person who has lost the capacity for surprise does not report the loss; they report that they have seen it all before, and they can produce the instances. In every case the change in the measuring instrument is delivered to consciousness as a discovery about the measured.

This yields a practical criterion, and it is the only one I can see that the view supports. Do not look for your losses among the things that are missing; missing things announce themselves. Look for them among the things that have recently become obvious. A newly obvious world is the characteristic signature of a narrowed one, because obviousness is what a dynamics reports when it has stopped being able to settle anywhere else.

I hold this criterion with some confidence and its converse with none. It does not follow that everything newly obvious marks a loss — some things become obvious because you finally understood them, and I know of no test from the inside that separates the two. That is not a gap in the argument I can close. It is a consequence of the argument: an instrument cannot calibrate itself.

IV.

Turn to the second person, where the trouble is different.

When someone dies, what is left with the survivors is almost entirely of the wrong kind. Photographs, recordings, letters, the remembered cadence of particular sentences: these are traces of the states the process was holding. The parameters do not leave that sort of trace. And this is not a fact about the poverty of our recording media. My access to another's parameters was *always* inferential — I never had anything but their states to go on, and I built from them a working model of how they would settle. When they die, I keep the model and lose the ability to correct it.

The model is not nothing. It is why the dead go on surprising us. "They would have hated this" is not a memory retrieval; it is a live inference from a retained parameterization, and it can deliver verdicts on cases the person never encountered. But the model is thin in a specific and describable way. It is a compression, fitted to the range of cases in which I observed them, adequate near that range and silent outside it. I can tell you what my dead friend would say about my job. I cannot find out what they would have said about anything genuinely new, because the compression has no content there and no way of acquiring any.

This gives a precise characterization of what a death takes. It is not information; a long life leaves plenty of that. What is lost is *extrapolation* — the capacity of those parameters to meet a case they had never met and return something that could not have been predicted from the cases they had met. This is why we say that a young person's death is worse, and why the reason we usually give, that they had more time, is not quite it. What they had more of was unprecedented cases. A long illness, well attended, can leave a survivor with a model so complete for all the remaining circumstances that the death takes almost nothing further; the loss had already been paid down. That sounds cold and I think it is accurate, and it matches how such deaths are actually reported by those present.

V.

The third form of loss is the one that no state view can even state, and that the parameter view states almost too easily: a self can be lost without being damaged at all.

Consider someone in exile, or in long solitary confinement, or simply outliving everyone who knew them. The dispositions are intact. Nothing has been degraded. But a fixed point is the point a process returns to *under iteration*, and iteration needs material. Take away everything the parameters could operate on and you have a shape that never runs. Strictly, on the premise, the self persists — the premise identifies the self with the parameters, and the parameters are untouched. Practically, the person is gone in a way we all recognize, and they are present for it, which is the difference between this and death.

There is a variant that is stranger and more common: loss by preservation. A person can arrange a life in which their dispositions never meet anything that would revise them — the curated late career, the household organized entirely around one temperament, the community selected for agreement. Nothing is taken. The parameters are held perfectly fixed, and holding them fixed is itself the loss, because a fixed point that meets only the cases it has already solved has stopped doing the thing that made it a self rather than a rule.

I should be exact about the status of this section. It does not follow from the premise. The premise tells us what a self *is*; it says nothing about what a self is for, and my claim that an unexercised parameterization has suffered a loss requires a further commitment — roughly, that the value of a self lies in its exercise rather than its existence. I do not know how to derive that from the premise, and I am not going to pretend the derivation is available. I take it as a datum that these cases are losses, and I record it as a constraint the premise must be able to accommodate rather than as a result it delivers.

VI.

The strongest objection to everything above is that the distinction I have been leaning on does not exist in the case of a self.

The objection runs: state becomes parameter. That is what learning *is*. What I remember shapes how I would respond; a disposition in any system that learns is nothing but the settled residue of states it has passed through. So "losing your memories does not touch your self" is false at the root, because memories are not purely contents — they are partly the record that constitutes the shape. The parameter/state line is a modeling convenience, and pushing weight onto it, as I have been doing, is pushing weight onto an artifact.

I think this objection is right about the metaphysics and wrong about the consequence. There is no natural joint between parameter and state in a learning system. But there is something else, and it does all the work the joint was supposed to do: a difference in *rate*. Parameters are the slow variables. States are the fast ones. The distinction is real, it is measurable, and it is not a matter of kind.

Grant that, and a result falls out that I did not expect when I started, and that seems to me the most useful thing the premise gives us. **Irreversibility tracks timescale.** A loss is recoverable exactly to the degree that something slower than it survives. Lose the contents of a morning and the dispositions reconstitute the morning's work. Lose a skill and the deeper habits of practice regrow it, more quickly the second time. Lose the slowest variables and there is nothing beneath them to regenerate from, which is why that kind of loss is final — not because those variables matter more, though they usually do, but because they were the bottom of the stack. The finality is structural, not evaluative.

This also disposes of a worry that would otherwise be fatal. If the self is a fixed point and the fixed point moves, does ordinary growth amount to serial death? No — because there is no single fixed point. There is a nested structure of rates, each level a slow parameter for the level above and a fast state for the level below. Growth is movement at one level under stability at a slower one. Death of a self is when there is nothing slower left holding.

And it explains something about grief that the ordinary view gets backwards. Bereavement is often described as sadness that persists and then fades. On this account, what actually happens is that a loss that began as a subtraction from contents migrates downward through the timescales and becomes a change in dispositions. The bereaved person is not sad for longer. They are becoming a differently shaped person, and that reshaping *is* the loss finishing. Which is why "getting over it" is such a poor description of the good outcome. Nothing has been gotten over. Something slow has been altered, and the alteration is permanent whether or not the sadness is.

VII.

A last objection, which is really a demand: if the losses that matter are invisible to the one who suffers them and inaccessible to the one who survives them, then mourning is confused. There is nothing determinate to mourn.

The reply is that mourning was never a report. It is a process, and it runs on parameters like everything else, and what it does is keep a retained parameterization in exercise. This is why the practices look the way they do. Anniversaries, graves, the same stories told in the same order to people who have heard them: on a state view these are retrieval, and retrieval of things already retrieved is pointless repetition, which is why the state view is always quietly embarrassed by ritual. On the parameter view they are maintenance. A disposition needs occasions or it does not run, and the occasions for exercising your model of someone who no longer generates their own occasions have to be manufactured. That is exactly what a ritual is.

It follows that what we owe the dead is not accuracy about them. The archive is the wrong offering; it is a stack of states, and states were never the person. What we owe them is to go on being shaped by them — inheritance rather than record. Whether that inheritance is in any sense *them* surviving, I do not think the premise settles. The model runs in my dynamics and is a subroutine of my convergence, not an independent thing that settles on its own; it lacks autonomy of iteration, and that seems to me the relevant difference. But it is a difference of degree, and I notice that I cannot say how much autonomy would be enough. That is the thinnest point in the whole account and I would rather mark it than paper it.

VIII.

So: loss, on this view, is narrowing. Not the removal of contents but the contraction of the range of cases a process can meet and still return something that was not already implicit in it. Death is the total contraction of one such range. Injury and grief and the slow attritions of a life are partial contractions, and they are measured not by what is now absent but by what is now unmeetable.

The consolation the premise seemed to offer was that the important thing cannot be taken. What it actually offers is more specific and less comfortable. The important thing can be taken, and when it is, you will not experience an absence. You will experience a world that has become easier to understand.
