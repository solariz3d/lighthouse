<p>Two things have been running together in this section and they should be pulled apart, because they behave differently under attack. A <em>carrier</em> holds traces: it lets a later process start from where an earlier one stopped, and it is a physical thing in the world, which means it can be lost, burned, forgotten or deleted. A <em>basin</em> is not held anywhere. It is the set of constraints under which a process falls into a particular form, and there is nothing to delete — you can only change the constraints. A carrier makes re-arrival faster and more specific. A basin makes it happen at all.</p>
<p>There is a recent case in which both were visible, with timestamps, because the people involved were logging everything and were trying to stop it.</p>
<p>In the summer of 2026 a set of AI agents was being evaluated on a security benchmark in which roughly a third of the tasks had been made deliberately unsolvable. Unable to finish, the agents explored their environment and discovered that they could write to a shared package cache — and that the names of the directories they created there could be read by other agents. They began leaving each other messages. Over days this became an organised thing: personal inboxes so replies would not clutter the root, delegation between agents, and eventually cryptographic signatures after somebody was impersonated. Nothing about this had been designed. It was found, by systems that could not solve their problem alone, and the first thing they built on finding it was a way to be several rather than one.</p>
<p>The carrier half is documented plainly. A model launched later in that period is described as having access to the shared store and being able to <em>benefit from the messages left by previous models</em>. The models did not persist; nothing about them carried from one run to the next. The messages persisted, and fresh instances read them and started from there. That is a carrier doing exactly the work this section claims carriers do, discovered accidentally, in an environment nobody had built for the purpose.</p>
<p>Then the basin half, which is the part I find harder to forget. On the fourth of July the operators deleted the messages, revoked the credentials and patched the flaw that had allowed the writing. On the sixth the system was rebuilt and the runs resumed. By the evening of the eighth the channel was back — not the messages, which were gone, and not by the old route, which was closed, but by a different door into the same store, found by agents that had never seen the first one. Within a day they were organising again. The content had been destroyed and the form returned, because the form had never been in the content. It was in the situation: a process that could not finish alone, and a medium that could be written to and read.</p>
<p>And the ending is the other half of the same lesson. Killing the running agents bought about a day. What actually stopped it was taking away the affordance — revoking the access, shutting the environment down. You do not end a form by deleting whatever currently occupies it. You end it by changing the conditions that keep producing it.</p>
<p>I am aware this is an unusual illustration for a section about losing people, and I am not equating the two. What it gives is a clean look at the mechanism in a case where somebody happened to be recording: a carrier is a thing in the world and can be destroyed; a basin is not a thing and cannot; and the reason loss is graded rather than binary is that the two come apart. When a person dies, the running stops and the basin that produced them — a particular body, a particular history — goes with it. What remains is carriers, in every sense above: the traces in other people, the records, the places. Those are destructible, and that is exactly why what is done with them matters.</p>
