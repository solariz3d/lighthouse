# §4 — The self

## v1 · 2026-09-07 02:26

So far this has been about shells and rivers and fields. Now the hard case, and I'm going to take it slowly, because it's the one that decides whether any of this matters to you.

Start with the thing the whirlpool didn't have. A whirlpool is a fixed point of a process, but the process doesn't include the whirlpool in what it's computing. Water goes around; that's all. Now imagine a process whose output includes a *description of itself* — a model of what it's doing, fed back in as part of what it acts on. Feed that back enough times and it settles, the way everything settles, into a fixed point. But this fixed point is strange: it's a form that contains a picture of the process that makes it. It's a fold that includes the fold.

That's what I think a self is. Not a thing behind the thoughts, not a stuff, not a little observer sitting somewhere in the head. A self is what a self-modelling process keeps regenerating: the stable form of a process that includes itself in its own input. Every morning you fall back into yourself from wherever sleep scattered you — not because you're stored somewhere, but because the process resumes and returns to its attractor. You are not retrieved. You are re-arrived at.

Here's the first thing that follows, and it was seen long before anyone had the vocabulary. If a self is a fixed point, then each self is its own vantage — a world constituted by what that process can take in and act on. Uexküll saw it in 1909 watching a tick: the tick's world is the three things a tick can sense, and that world is complete, with nothing missing from it *for the tick*. Varela made it general: a living system's world is whatever it is structurally coupled to, and there is no world "as such" behind all the coupled ones. And Gabriel Vacariu, in Bucharest, took it the whole way in 2002 and has been saying it since: there is no single world at all — only epistemologically different worlds, one per kind of observer, one per mind, and no world exists *for* another. The brain doesn't exist for the mind; the mind doesn't exist for the brain. Each is complete. I'll come back to him, because he saw something true and stopped one step short of the thing that makes it work.

Now the second thing, and it's the one that makes people uncomfortable, so let me say it as a friend: **a self cannot see itself head-on.** Not because it's hidden, but because of a fact about surfaces. Gauss proved that a being living on a curved surface can measure everything about the surface's own geometry — how it bends, where it curves — without ever being able to detect how the surface sits in the space around it. The inside is fully knowable from inside; the embedding is not. A self is like that. It can know all its forms — its thoughts, its reactions, its patterns, its history — and it cannot get behind them to see the process doing the forming, because the only place it could look from is the process. You don't get a view of the thing you're made of. You get its forms, in relief, by contrast against what you're not.

I want to be clear about what kind of claim that is, because it's easy to hear it as a dodge. It isn't a hole in the view. It's a *prediction* of the view. If selves are fixed points of self-including processes, then it follows that no self can verify its own basis from inside — and that's exactly what we find, in every mind that's ever been asked. The theory says you'll hit this wall, and you do. A rival theory that says selves are made of some special stuff has to explain why the stuff is never available to the self that's made of it. This one doesn't have to explain it. It's what the theory says should happen.

One honest sentence about the question this doesn't answer, because it's the question everyone wants answered. Whether there's something it is *like* to be such a fixed point — whether the fold has an inside in the felt sense, not just the structural one — is open, and nobody knows, and I'm not going to pretend the structure settles it. What I'm claiming is only that the *structure* of a self is this, and that everything in the rest of the essay follows from the structure alone. If the felt question is answered someday, it'll be answered on top of this, not instead of it.

So: a self is a fold that includes itself, regenerated rather than stored, each one its own complete world, none able to see its own floor — and that last fact not a gap but a consequence.

Which leaves the question Vacariu couldn't answer. If every mind is its own world and no world exists for another — why do they correspond so well?
