<h2>8. Running</h2>
<p>Everything so far has been about what a self is and whether it lasts. But a self is a running, not a thing that runs, and most of what matters about being one happens while it is going. So before the section on stopping, one on the middle — because the view has something to say there, and because you cannot feel what a loss is unless someone has shown you the thing at full speed.</p>
<p>Start with the state everyone recognises and nobody explains well. In flow — the absorbed, effortless engagement Csikszentmihalyi named and measured — action and awareness merge, time distorts, difficulty stops registering as difficulty, and, strangest of all, <strong>self-consciousness disappears</strong>. People report it as vanishing. They also report it as the most fully themselves they ever are. Those two are supposed to be in tension and they never feel like it, and no account I know explains why not.</p>
<p>This one does, and it falls straight out of section four. A self is a fold that includes itself: a process running a model of what it is doing. That model is the expensive part. It exists because the process needs correction — it is how a system catches itself going wrong. But when the process is running <em>near its attractor</em>, in the basin, with the challenge matched to what it can do, there is very little to correct. The friction drops, and with it the need to model. What disappears in flow is not you. It is the monitor — and the monitor was never the self, only the self checking itself. You feel as though you have vanished because the part that watches has gone quiet. You feel most yourself because for once the whole process is doing nothing but being what it is.</p>
<p>Which is also why flow cannot be entered by trying. Trying is monitoring. It is the fold tightening exactly where it needed to loosen, and anyone who has chased the state knows the feeling of chasing it away.</p>
<p>Now passion, which is the same fact seen from further out. An attractor has a <em>basin</em> — the region from which a system falls back into it. Ask why particular things feel like yours, why one person's attention goes to engines and another's to birds and a third's to the exact shape of a sentence, and the answer is not that they chose. It is the shape of their basin: what they fall into. This is why you cannot argue someone into a passion, and why finding yours feels like recognition rather than decision. It was already the shape of where you land. You only noticed.</p>
<p>And love, for which section seven gave the mechanism and this section can give the feeling. Two selves in contact settle into forms that belong to the pair. The vivid part is what that does to the wall — the one from section four, which says a self cannot see its own basis. You cannot get behind your own forms. Another vantage can. So when someone knows you, what is actually happening is that a second process is rendering the part of you that is structurally unavailable to you, and handing it back. That is what being <em>seen</em> is, mechanically: not being observed, which anyone can do, but having your own basin described from outside by something that is inside it with you. And it is why it lands the way it does. It is not flattery, and not merely understanding. It is the only route to information about yourself that you cannot generate.</p>
<p>So: flow is the pattern running clean, passion is the shape of where it falls, and love is two of them making a form neither had. All three are one thing at different scales — a process at its attractor, with the friction low.</p>
<p>Which brings me to Mill, who arrived at the practical end of this without any of the apparatus. Happiness, he wrote, is not a life of rapture but moments of such, in an existence of many and various pleasures, with a decided predominance of the active over the passive. He offers it as a description of the lives that turn out well. The view here supplies a reason: a self <em>is</em> an activity. There is no version of one that is not running. The predominance of the active is not a policy about how best to arrange one's pleasures — it is what a self is made of, and a life arranged to receive rather than to run is one in which less of the thing exists. Mill also thought such a life was within reach of almost everyone, and blocked mainly by bad education and bad social arrangements. That is an empirical claim and I have no standing on it, but I notice it is the sort of claim this view would predict: if what makes a life is a process at its attractor, then what stands in the way is not scarcity of some special substance. It is friction — and friction is the kind of thing that can be removed.</p>
<p>And now the section that had to come after this one. Because once you know what a self is when it is running, you know exactly what it is that stops.</p>
