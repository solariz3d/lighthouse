<p>One refinement, because the kind of attractor turns out to matter. A self is not a <em>limit cycle</em> — the sort of attractor that repeats, running the same states in the same order forever. You have never thought the same thought twice in exactly the same way, and you would not want to. What you do is return to yourself without recurring: bounded, stable, recognisably one thing across decades, and never once in the same state. The mathematical name for an attractor with those properties is a <em>strange attractor</em>, and the description fits so exactly that I think it is the right term rather than a borrowed one. It is also the answer to a question the earlier sections raise and do not settle: how a thing can be reliably itself and never repeat.</p>
<p>And it means the principle of the last section — that non-repetition is armour — reaches the self as well, which is where it stops being an abstraction. Periodicity in a person is pathology, and the clinical vocabulary says so without meaning to: <em>rumination</em>, <em>intrusive</em>, <em>obsessive</em>, <em>stuck</em>, <em>loop</em>. The thought that arrives identical every time. The argument that runs the same course every time it starts. At the extreme, a seizure is a brain whose populations have fallen into lockstep, which is to say a brain that has become periodic. A self that repeats is trapped, and trapped is what it feels like from the inside. Health, in a process, looks like coming back without coming round again.</p>
<p>There is a further fact here that I find striking and will not lean on. In the study of dynamical systems under perturbation, the structures that survive longest are those whose frequency ratios are hardest to approximate by fractions, and the most robust of all — the last to break as the perturbation grows — is the one with the golden mean as its ratio. That is a theorem-shaped result about what endures disturbance, and its answer is the same constant that solves the sunflower's problem. I am not claiming that a self has a golden-mean winding number; that step is where this line of thought turns into numerology, and it turns into numerology often. I note only that when mathematics is asked <em>what survives perturbation</em> — which is this essay's question, asked in the one place it can be answered exactly — the answer has the same form as the answer everywhere else in this section.</p>
