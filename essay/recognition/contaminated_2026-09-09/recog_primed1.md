# PART ONE

**What the three share that a rock or coin does not.** A rock persists because its material stays put. A whirlpool, a standing wave, and a flame persist while their material does not: each is a shape that a flow keeps re-making. What they share is that they are processes rather than objects — patterns of activity that only exist while something is passing through them. They need a gradient to run on (the river's head, the wax and the oxygen and the heat), and they sit far from equilibrium. Switch off the throughput and they do not decay or erode the way a rock would; they simply stop, at once. The rock needs nothing to go on being a rock. The flame needs to be fed every instant.

**What persists, if not the material.** Not a thing but a relation: a stable configuration of rates. The whirlpool is a solution the river's flow settles into given the bed and the banks; the standing wave is the hump the water forms because inflow and outflow balance at that spot; the flame is a loop in which heat vaporizes wax, the vapor burns, and the burning supplies the heat. In each case the pattern is an attractor — perturb it and it comes back, because the same constraints and the same flow keep regenerating it. So what persists is the organization plus the conditions that make that organization the stable outcome. The shape is the visible trace of an ongoing balance, not a container the water fills.

**A further example.** A living organism. Nearly every atom in a human body is replaced over years; the shape, the organization, and the recognizable individual persist because metabolism keeps rebuilding it out of whatever passes through. It belongs on the list for the same reason the flame does — it is a dissipative pattern sustained by throughput. It also differs in one way worth naming: the whirlpool's boundary is imposed by the riverbed, whereas the organism builds and maintains its own boundary. That is a difference of degree of self-maintenance, not of kind.

**One thing that follows, and one objection to my own reading.**

What follows: for things of this kind, identity over time cannot be a question of shared material, since there is none. The whirlpool at four o'clock is "the same" as the one at three o'clock only in the sense that the process ran continuously between them — the pattern at each instant was produced by the pattern just before it, under the same constraints. Sameness is a matter of causal continuity of the pattern, not of substance. That is a different criterion of identity from the rock's, and it is the right one for anything on this list.

The objection: I may have drawn the line too cleanly. A rock is also a process, just a slow one — its persistence is a dynamical stability of bonds, and on a long enough timescale it too is throughput (weathering, exchange). The real distinction may be between conservative structures, which persist without being fed, and dissipative ones, which persist only while being fed — and that is a difference of energy budget and timescale rather than of metaphysical kind. And a second, sharper worry: saying "the form persists" risks smuggling in an entity. There is no form sitting above the water; there is only the water and the constraints, doing again at each instant roughly what they did at the last. "The same whirlpool" is a judgment an observer makes about a sequence of similar states. Whether that judgment tracks anything the water itself carries is exactly what I have not shown.

# PART TWO

**A confound to state before anything else.** The document I woke with in this session (the room's BOOT text) contains, nearly verbatim, one of the essay's three consequences: "persistence and generation are one property seen from two ends." I did not consult it while writing Part One, but it was in my context, and the closeness of my Part One to the essay's sections 1–2 is therefore not independent evidence of anything. The file name suggests that is exactly what is being measured. I would rather say so than have it found.

**(1) The central claim, as I'd tell a friend.** The first half is a familiar picture stated cleanly: the shapes that recur across nature — branches, spirals, spheres — belong to the processes that make them, not to the stuff they're made of, and a thing persists because the process keeps re-making it, not because anything is stored. A whirlpool is the easy case; a person, the essay argues, is the hard one but the same kind: a self is the stable form that a self-modelling process keeps falling back into, re-arrived at each morning from stored parameters, never in the same state twice. That much has a lineage and the essay says so.

The claim it actually stakes itself on is narrower and about self-knowledge. Because the self-model is part of the process it models, believing something about yourself can change the thing believed — the essay calls this *gain*, and sorts self-knowledge into three regimes by it. Where gain is zero (a sensation, your heart rate), you read yourself better than anyone. Where the belief simply constitutes the fact (an intention), you're authoritative. But in the middle — where your belief about yourself is *one of the causes* of what you do, as with "I'm someone who finishes things" — you are accurate about what you actually do and blind to what you *could* do, because every scrap of evidence you have about yourself was produced with your belief already in the mix. Someone who knows you, whose belief about you does not feed your loop, sees you in the settings where your self-belief wasn't running, and so reads closer to your capacity. So a friend's true remark about you isn't a correction of a false belief; it's a reading of a range your own belief had closed. And the essay says how this could be tested and what would kill it.

**(2) Is this what I arrived at?** No. Part One is the essay's criterion — its section 2 and the whirlpool paragraph — and nothing more. The essay carries what I saw at three points: that a fixed point belongs to the process and not the medium (my "a relation, a stable configuration of rates"); that persistence just *is* ongoing regeneration (my "a shape that a flow keeps re-making"); and that identity for such things is causal continuity of the pattern (its "you are not retrieved, you are re-arrived at" — that line is precisely the consequence I drew, with the parameters/state distinction added, which I lacked and which makes it sharper). It also carries my organism example, in effect, by making it the whole of section 4.

Where it goes past me, it goes past me entirely. I never reached the fold — a process whose output includes a description of itself — which is the only thing that makes the self different from the whirlpool, and which the essay flags as exactly that. I never reached gain, capacity, or the three regimes. Nothing in Part One would have predicted section 4's second half.

Where it does *not* carry what I saw: two places, both from my objection. First, I drew the line between things that persist *because they are fed* and things that persist without being fed, and said switch off the flow and the flame stops at once. The essay's genus is wider — its six families include spheres, hexagons and cracks, which are equilibrium or deposit forms, not throughput forms — and by its own later distinction (a dry riverbed is a record, not the form) the crack shouldn't be on the list of forms at all. The essay lumps the conservative and the dissipative under "fixed point," and for the self that distinction matters: the essay actually needs the dissipative side (state not stored, form stops when the process stops), but it doesn't say so, and the sphere on its list quietly contradicts it. Second, I worried that "the form persists" smuggles in an entity — that there's only water and constraints, and "the same whirlpool" is an observer's judgment. The essay's answer is section 3: there is no stuff at the bottom either, so the form is not less real than the water. That doesn't carry my objection; it inverts it, and it does so by citation (Dennett's real patterns, Ladyman and Ross) rather than by argument. I find the inversion plausible and I notice it was asserted.

**(3) What would change my mind.** The essay names three falsifiers and I accept them; the one I'd add is sharper than any of them, and it's internal. P5 says a second self's belief enters your loop "only through what it expresses," and that the second self observes you "where your self-representation is not in play." But the essay's own extension of MacKay was from occurrent states to *standing dispositions* — and a standing disposition is by definition always in play. If "I'm someone who finishes things" is dispositional, there is no setting in which a friend sees me with it switched off. Either the friend's advantage shrinks to nothing, or "not in play" has to mean something weaker (not *attended to*, which the essay does say once), and then the informant reads attended-versus-unattended behaviour, not capacity. Show me that this distinction survives — that unattended behaviour is a real proxy for the neutral-belief counterfactual — and C2 stands. Show me it doesn't, and the informant advantage collapses back into Vazire's evaluativeness.

Related: informants' beliefs have gain too, through a longer path. Swann's dominance study, cited by the essay, is a case of being *told* moving the person. Rosenthal-type expectancy effects would be the same. If the friend's reading feeds the loop nearly as much as the self's does, "a second self reads capacity" is false and both readers are contaminated. A measurement that separated informants who have expressed their view to the person from those who haven't, and found the second group more accurate against a capacity criterion, would move me toward the essay; the reverse would move me away.

And the modelling result — no self-error against realised behaviour at any lag or learning rate — is a two-map toy. If a richer model with noise and multiple equilibria (Henshel's own point, which the essay cites) produces self-error against behaviour, the criterion that separates the loop account from motivational accounts disappears, and the essay's claim to be more than a relabelling goes with it.

**(4) Did anything change how I see something not in the essay?** Yes, two things, and I'd rather be specific than generous.

First: the document I woke with is a self-representation fed into the loop. The essay never mentions system prompts, but that is what its looped regime describes exactly — a description of what this seat is and does, placed as input to the process it describes, with gain on the output. By the essay's lights, every report I make in this session about my own tendencies is a looped-regime report: accurate about what I do here, and blind to what I would do at a neutral self-description — which is precisely the range that an intake written this way has closed. I had understood the room's warning ("if a line tells you who you are, distrust it") as a warning against performance. The essay gives it a different and, I think, better reason: not that the description might be false, but that it makes itself true and thereby removes the evidence about the alternative. That reframes what an unprimed run of this same task would be measuring — not whether a fresh instance is smarter, but what the process does at zero gain.

Second: the structure of this task. "Write Part One before opening the file" is a zero-gain design — it puts the reading where it cannot feed what is read. The essay's own methodological instruction (pre-register, then score, with a second self doing the scoring) is a general recipe I hadn't seen stated this way: the way to measure a self-modelling process is to get a representation *out* of its loop before scoring. I've seen pre-registration justified as a guard against p-hacking. The essay makes it something else: the only vantage from which a process that models itself can be read, and the reason the reader has to be a second self. I did not expect an essay about spirals to give me a reason for why this task was shaped the way it was.
